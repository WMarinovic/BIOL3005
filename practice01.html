Scenario 1
Measuring a Decision: Donders’ Subtractive Method
Why this matters for psychologists

Reaction time is still the most widely used measure in cognitive psychology and in clinical assessment. Neuropsychologists, sports scientists and road-safety researchers all use slowed responses to infer that something has gone wrong, but a slow response on its own does not say where in the chain from stimulus to action the delay arose. The subtractive logic Donders introduced is what lets a researcher or clinician ask whether a person is slow to perceive, slow to decide or slow to move, and those answers point to quite different problems and interventions.

A research team replicated Donders’ experiment with 40 undergraduate participants. Each participant completed three tasks with the same two lights (one on the left, one on the right) and the same response keys:

  • Simple task: press one key as soon as any light appears.
  • Go/no-go task: press the key only when the left light appears, and do nothing when the right light appears.
  • Choice task: press the left key for the left light and the right key for the right light.

The graph shows mean reaction times (ms) for correct responses in each task. To save time, every participant completed the tasks in the same order: simple, then go/no-go, then choice.

Easy
a) Which mental operations does each of the three tasks involve? Explain why the tasks are built so that each one adds exactly one operation to the one before.

Start with the simplest task and ask what has to happen between the light appearing and the key press. Then ask what the go/no-go task requires that the simple task does not, and what the choice task requires that the go/no-go task does not.

The simple task involves only detecting (sensing) the stimulus and executing the motor response. The participant does not need to know which light appeared, only that a light appeared.

The go/no-go task adds one operation: stimulus identification. The participant must work out whether the light is the left one before deciding to respond or withhold. There is still only one possible response, so no response has to be selected.

The choice task adds a further operation: response selection. The participant must identify the light and then choose which of two keys to press.

The tasks are built as a ladder, each containing everything in the one below plus one extra stage, because that is what allows subtraction to isolate a single operation. If two tasks differed in more than one way, the difference in their reaction times could not be attributed to any one stage.

Easy
b) Using the means in the graph, estimate the duration of stimulus identification, the duration of response selection, and the total time Donders would have treated as the time taken to decide.

Each estimate is a subtraction between two adjacent tasks, or between the first and last. Decide which pair of tasks differs only in the stage you want.

Stimulus identification = go/no-go − simple = 280 − 220 = 60 ms.

Response selection = choice − go/no-go = 320 − 280 = 40 ms.

Decision time (identification plus selection) = choice − simple = 320 − 220 = 100 ms, which matches the order of magnitude Donders reported. Note that the two component estimates add up to the total, which is exactly what the method assumes they should do.

Medium
c) The subtractive method rests on the assumption of pure insertion. State the assumption, and describe one way it could fail in this experiment. What would a failure do to the estimates?

Pure insertion is a claim about what happens to the other stages when you add a new one. Think about whether having to choose between two keys might change how a person detects the light or prepares the movement, not just add a stage on top.

Pure insertion is the assumption that adding a stage leaves all the other stages unchanged, so that the components of a task simply add up. The subtraction then measures the inserted stage and nothing else.

It could fail if inserting an operation changes how the shared stages run. For example, in the simple task participants can prepare the single movement in advance and launch it the moment anything appears; in the choice task they cannot prepare a specific movement until they know which key is needed, so the motor stage itself may be slower, not just preceded by an extra decision. Equally, knowing that the light must be identified might lead participants to wait for a clearer sensory signal before responding.

If the shared stages change, the difference in reaction times mixes the duration of the inserted stage with the change in the others. The 40 ms and 60 ms estimates could then be too large or too small, and there would be no way to tell from the reaction times alone.

Medium
d) Every participant completed the tasks in the same order (simple, go/no-go, choice). Explain why this is a confound, how it could bias the stage estimates in each direction, and how the design should be changed.

A confound is anything that varies alongside the variable you care about. What else, apart from task, changes between the first and third task in a fixed order? Consider both practice and fatigue.

Task is perfectly confounded with order. Anything that changes with time in the session, rather than with the task itself, is mixed into the reaction-time differences.

Practice makes people faster as the session goes on. Because the choice task always came last, practice would shorten its reaction time and underestimate the stage durations. Fatigue or boredom would do the opposite, lengthening later tasks and overestimating them. The researchers cannot know which influence dominated, so the 60 ms and 40 ms values may be biased in an unknown direction.

The fix is to counterbalance the order across participants (for example, each of the six possible orders used equally often), or to interleave short blocks of the three tasks, so that practice and fatigue are spread evenly across tasks rather than piled onto one of them.

Hard
e) In a follow-up study, single TMS pulses were delivered during the choice task at different times after the light. Pulses over anterior premotor cortex slowed responses most at about 140 ms, over posterior premotor cortex at about 180 ms, and over sensorimotor cortex from about 220 ms. What does this approach reveal that Donders’ method cannot, and what are its main limitations?

Donders estimates how long a stage lasts from behaviour alone. TMS intervenes in the brain at a chosen moment. Think about what kind of claim each method supports (correlational or causal) and what it says about the order and location of stages. Then list what TMS cannot reach or cannot specify.

Donders’ method infers how long hidden stages take, but only from behaviour and only under the pure-insertion assumption. It says nothing about where in the brain each stage happens or whether a particular region is necessary for it.

Single-pulse TMS produces a brief, reversible virtual lesion. Because the pulse can be timed, the study shows when each region is doing necessary work: anterior premotor cortex around 140 ms, posterior premotor around 180 ms, sensorimotor cortex from 220 ms. This is causal chronometry: a processing cascade whose order is established by interrupting it rather than inferred, in healthy people serving as their own controls. It shows that these regions are not merely active during the task but necessary at particular moments.

The limitations are real. The depth and spread of stimulation cannot be specified precisely, so effects attributed to one site may reflect activity spreading to connected regions. Only surface cortex can be reached, so deep structures such as the basal ganglia are out of range. Stimulation can sometimes facilitate rather than disrupt performance. And repetitive TMS carries a seizure risk, which is why its use is governed by safety guidelines and ethics approval.

Applied
f) A sports physician tests a footballer after a head knock. Her simple RT is 300 ms, go/no-go RT 365 ms and choice RT 410 ms; her pre-season values were close to the class means in the graph. Using the subtractive logic, where does her slowing appear to lie, and what caution should the physician attach to that conclusion?

Compute her identification and selection times and compare them with the values you calculated in part b. Then ask which stage the extra time must be in, and remember the assumption from part c.

Her stage estimates are: identification = 365 − 300 = 65 ms and response selection = 410 − 365 = 45 ms, very close to the 60 ms and 40 ms from the group data. Her decision time (110 ms) is therefore near normal. The slowing, about 80 ms, is already present in the simple task and carries through unchanged to the other two.

On the subtractive logic, the delay lies in the stages shared by all three tasks, detecting the stimulus or executing the movement, rather than in identifying the light or choosing a response. That points the physician towards sensory or motor processing rather than decision-making.

The caution is pure insertion. The conclusion holds only if her injury has not changed how the stages interact, for example by making her more cautious in every task. A single test session is also noisy, so the physician should repeat the testing, compare against her own baseline, and combine it with other clinical measures before drawing conclusions about her readiness to return to play.

Scenario 2
Dissociations, Imaging and TMS: Localising Language
Why this matters for psychologists

Clinical neuropsychologists assess people after stroke or injury and must decide which functions are impaired and why. The logic of dissociations determines what a pattern of test scores can and cannot tell them, and the strengths and limits of imaging and stimulation methods determine how far brain scans and research findings can be trusted when advising patients, families and courts.

A neuropsychology clinic assessed three patients after left-hemisphere strokes, together with 20 age-matched controls. Everyone completed two tasks:

  • Production: describing pictures in full sentences (percentage of sentences that were grammatically complete and fluent).
  • Comprehension: matching spoken sentences to the correct picture from a set of four (percentage correct).

Patient A had damage to the left inferior frontal lobe. Patient B had damage to the left posterior temporal lobe. Patient C had a large stroke affecting much of the left hemisphere. The graph shows each person’s scores, with the control mean.

Easy
a) Define a single dissociation and a double dissociation. Which patients in this data set together form a double dissociation?

A dissociation compares two functions within the same person. Look for one patient who is impaired on one task but not the other, and a second patient who shows the mirror image.

A single dissociation is when damage impairs one function (A) while leaving another (B) intact in the same person. A double dissociation is a pair of complementary findings: damage in one place impairs A but spares B, while damage elsewhere impairs B but spares A.

Patients A and B form a double dissociation. Patient A is poor at production (41%) but near normal at comprehension (90%). Patient B shows the reverse: near-normal production (88%) but very poor comprehension (37%).

Easy
b) Which classic aphasia does each of Patients A and B resemble? Describe what you would expect their everyday speech to sound like.

Think about where Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas are, and which function is lost after damage to each.

Patient A resembles Broca’s aphasia. The damage is in the left frontal lobe, where Broca’s area lies, and production is impaired while comprehension of everyday language is largely preserved. Their speech would be halting and effortful, with short, simplified utterances, even though they understand much of what is said to them.

Patient B resembles Wernicke’s aphasia. The damage is in the left temporal lobe, where Wernicke’s area lies, and comprehension is impaired while speech remains fluent. Their speech would flow easily and sound grammatical but carry little meaning, and they would have difficulty understanding others.

Medium
c) Suppose the clinic had seen only Patient A. Why would that single case be weak evidence that production and comprehension depend on separate brain regions? What does Patient C add, or fail to add, to the argument?

Ask what else, apart from separate mechanisms, could make one task suffer more than the other after brain damage. Then look at whether Patient C’s pattern is a selective deficit at all.

A single dissociation shows only that one ability can be lost while another survives. That could happen because the two abilities rely on one shared mechanism and the impaired task is simply harder or more fragile: any damage might hit the more demanding task first. Patient A alone cannot rule this out, because we never see the other task fail while production survives.

The double dissociation with Patient B removes that explanation. If production were just the harder task, no patient should lose comprehension while keeping production, yet Patient B does exactly that. Each ability can be lost while the other is spared, so they cannot be one function riding on one mechanism.

Patient C adds little to the localisation argument. Both scores are low, so the pattern is a general impairment rather than a selective one, which is what a large lesion affecting many regions would be expected to produce. A case like C is consistent with both separate and shared mechanisms, so it cannot discriminate between them. It is a useful reminder that general damage is the default explanation the double dissociation has to defeat.

Medium
d) An fMRI study of healthy participants finds that both the left inferior frontal region and the left posterior temporal region are active during the comprehension task. A student concludes that “the lesion evidence must be wrong, because both areas do comprehension”. Evaluate this conclusion.

Two separate points are needed. First, what kind of evidence is fMRI activation: correlational or causal? Second, does “one region is necessary for a function” conflict with “many regions are active during it”? Think about networks.

The conclusion does not follow, for two reasons.

First, fMRI is correlational. It shows that a region is active while a task is performed, measured indirectly through the BOLD signal, but not that the region is necessary for the task. The frontal region could be active during comprehension because it is involved in something that accompanies comprehension (for example, preparing a possible response or inner speech) without being required for understanding. The lesion evidence asks a different question, what is lost when a region is damaged, and Patient A shows that comprehension largely survives frontal damage.

Second, localisation and distributed processing are complementary, not rival, claims. Most functions are carried out by networks of interconnected regions, so it is expected that several areas will be active during comprehension. A function can depend critically on one region and still be realised across a network that includes others. The imaging and lesion results are therefore answers to different questions, and both can be true.

Hard
e) Design a TMS experiment in healthy participants to test whether the left inferior frontal region is necessary for sentence production. Specify the conditions and controls, state the result that would support necessity, and identify two limitations of the inference.

A good design needs a comparison that rules out non-specific effects of stimulation (noise, discomfort, distraction) and shows that the effect is specific to one function. Think about sham stimulation, a control site and the comprehension task as a control task.

Healthy participants would perform both the production and comprehension tasks while receiving TMS in separate, counterbalanced sessions to (1) the left inferior frontal region, (2) a control site such as the left posterior temporal region or a region not involved in language, and (3) sham stimulation, which mimics the sound and sensation of TMS without inducing a current in the cortex. Each participant serves as their own control.

Necessity would be supported by a selective result: frontal TMS impairs production (slower or less fluent sentences) relative to sham and to the control site, while leaving comprehension largely unaffected. Ideally temporal TMS would show the reverse, reproducing the double dissociation in healthy brains. A general slowing on both tasks after any active stimulation would point instead to non-specific effects.

Limitations: the spatial spread and depth of the induced current are not precisely known, so an effect attributed to the frontal site might reflect disruption spreading to connected regions. TMS reaches only surface cortex, so it cannot test deeper contributors. Stimulation can sometimes facilitate rather than impair, which complicates interpretation. Repetitive TMS also carries a small seizure risk, so the protocol would need safety screening and ethics approval specifying intensity and train duration.

Applied
f) A news headline reads: “Scans show people with a larger Broca’s area are more persuasive speakers.” As a psychologist asked to comment on the radio, explain what kind of study this almost certainly was and give at least two reasons why it does not show that a larger Broca’s area makes people more persuasive.

Nobody can randomly assign people to have larger or smaller brain regions. What does that make the design? Then consider the direction of the relationship and whether a third variable could produce both.

The study is almost certainly correlational: researchers measured the size of a brain region and a speaking measure in the same people and found that they go together. Nobody manipulated the size of Broca’s area, so random assignment was impossible.

At least three reasons block the causal reading. Direction: the relationship could run the other way. On Hebb’s principle, repeatedly used connections strengthen, so years of public speaking might change the region rather than the region producing the speaker. Third variables: education, practice, profession or general verbal ability could make people both better speakers and different in brain structure. Measurement: “persuasive” is a complex social outcome depending on far more than speech production, and the size of a region is not the same as how well it functions within a network.

A fair summary for listeners would be that the finding is interesting and could guide further research, but it shows an association, not that changing the brain region would change anyone’s persuasiveness.
