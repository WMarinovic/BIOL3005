Short-Term and Working Memory

Week 3Chapter 3Tutorial toolsPractice problemsAnswers

Model answers to the 16 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 3. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbook

Question 1

What did the whole-report versus partial-report difference in Sperling's experiment reveal about the capacity and decay of iconic memory? Why could the participants not have been preparing just one row in advance?

Show the answerHide the answer

With whole report, people could name only four or five letters, which seemed to be all they had taken in. With partial report, a tone after the display cued one row, and people reported about three of its four letters. Since any row could be cued, about three-quarters of every row, and so most of the array, must have been available when the tone sounded. Iconic memory therefore has a large capacity. When the cue was delayed, partial-report performance fell to whole-report levels within about a second, showing very rapid decay: most of the array fades before it can be read out. Participants could not have prepared one row in advance because the cue came after the display and was unpredictable; preparing only one row would have produced good performance on a third of trials and poor performance on the rest, rather than consistently high accuracy on whichever row was cued.

Question 2

H.M. and K.F. had opposite memory deficits. Set out the double dissociation precisely, and explain why the pair is stronger evidence for separate stores than either case alone.

Show the answerHide the answer

H.M. had intact short-term memory (he could hold a short string of digits normally) but could not form new long-term memories after his medial temporal lobe surgery. K.F. had the reverse: a digit span of one or two items, but broadly functional long-term learning and retention. Each was normal on exactly what the other could not do. A single case, such as H.M. alone, could be explained as long-term memory simply being more fragile or demanding, so that general damage would affect it first. The crossed pair rules out that explanation, because no single dimension of overall memory strength can produce both patterns. The two stores must depend on separable systems.

Question 3

In the Brown–Peterson task, why must participants count backwards during the delay? Explain how Keppel and Underwood's trial-order finding challenges a decay account of the result.

Show the answerHide the answer

Counting backwards in threes occupies rehearsal, so participants cannot keep the trigram alive by repeating it; without that, the task would measure how long people can rehearse rather than how long information lasts unrehearsed. The Petersons attributed the steep forgetting to decay. Keppel and Underwood noticed that forgetting was minimal on the first trial and grew over later trials. If the cause were time-based decay, the first trial should look like any other, since the same time passes. What builds up across trials is similar material, so the better explanation is proactive interference: earlier trigrams compete with the current one at retrieval.

Question 4

Explain chunking and use it to interpret Chase and Simon's chess result. Why did the experts' advantage disappear for random boards, and what does that imply about the independence of the short-term and long-term stores?

Show the answerHide the answer

A chunk is a meaningful package of information that occupies one unit of short-term capacity; using knowledge to recode several items into one chunk, as when eight digits become a familiar date, lets more raw information be held. Chess masters reconstructed far more pieces than novices from briefly shown positions taken from real games, because their long-term store of familiar configurations let them encode several pieces as a single chunk. On random boards there were no familiar patterns to recognise, so the advantage almost vanished (only a small residual advantage appears in larger samples). The masters did not have larger short-term memories. This implies the short-term and long-term stores are not independent: what can be held in short-term memory depends on the contents of long-term memory.

Question 5

What does the Alvarez and Cavanagh result imply about whether short-term capacity is best measured in items or in information? Outline the slot and resource accounts and state how they differ.

Show the answerHide the answer

Alvarez and Cavanagh found that the number of objects people could hold fell as complexity increased, from about four coloured squares to fewer than two shaded cubes, suggesting capacity is set partly by information per item rather than by a fixed count, with a ceiling of about four or five for the simplest items. Awh, Barton and Vogel argued this reflected harder change detection for similar complex objects, and when changes were easy to detect, the number held was about constant. On a slot account, visual working memory holds a small fixed number of items at fixed resolution: an item is either stored or absent, and complexity costs slots. On a resource account, a continuous pool of precision is shared among any number of items, so holding more items makes each less precise, with no hard item limit. The debate remains unresolved.

Question 6

Describe the evidence for the phonological loop from the phonological similarity effect, the word-length effect, and articulatory suppression. Why does the word-length effect suggest a time-based rather than an item-based capacity, and why does articulatory suppression strengthen the case?

Show the answerHide the answer

Phonological similarity: lists of similar-sounding letters (b, d, g, p) are recalled worse than dissimilar ones even when read silently, so the store uses a sound-based code. Word length: lists of short words are recalled better than lists of long words with the same number of items. If capacity were a number of slots, length should not matter; that it does implies a time-based limit, about two seconds of speech, set by how much can be re-articulated before the earliest item fades. Articulatory suppression: repeating an irrelevant sound ties up the articulatory process and, for visual lists, abolishes both effects. This strengthens the case because it shows both effects depend specifically on the articulatory rehearsal process rather than on some general property of the words. With spoken lists, suppression removes the word-length effect but not the similarity effect, because heard speech reaches the sound store directly.

Question 7

What is the function of the central executive, and why was the episodic buffer added to the model? Refer to the chess-master result in your answer.

Show the answerHide the answer

The central executive is the control system: it directs attention, inhibits irrelevant material, plans and sequences, updates the contents of the stores and integrates their outputs, often summarised as shifting, updating and inhibition. The original model had no way to bind the verbal and visual stores together or to explain how long-term knowledge enters and structures working memory. Yet we hold far more of a meaningful sentence or a real chess position than of a random list. Chess masters remember real positions so well because long-term knowledge of configurations is shaping what they hold. Baddeley (2000) added the episodic buffer, a limited store run by the executive that integrates information across the subsystems and with long-term memory, to accommodate exactly this.

Question 8

Loading working memory can either protect you from distraction or expose you to it. Explain how that can be true of the same system, using the maintenance-versus-control distinction.

Show the answerHide the answer

Working memory has components that do different jobs. Loading maintenance, for example holding a set of coloured squares, occupies visual representational capacity and behaves like perceptual load: it reduces awareness of a peripheral distractor, protecting you from it. Loading control, as in the sandwich task with six digits or changing sequences, taxes the executive processes that keep task priorities straight, so irrelevant items win more often and distraction increases. The same system can therefore protect or expose you depending on which component is loaded, which is why "working memory load" has to be specified.

Question 9

How does Cowan's embedded-processes model differ from Baddeley's, and what kind of experimental result would favour one over the other?

Show the answerHide the answer

Baddeley's model treats working memory as a set of dedicated stores (loop, sketchpad, buffer) coordinated by an executive. Cowan's embedded-processes model treats it as the currently activated portion of long-term memory, with a narrower focus of attention holding about four chunks; there are no separate stores. For Baddeley, interference between two tasks depends on whether they recruit the same subsystem, so a visual and a verbal task should coexist well. For Cowan, interference depends on the similarity of the material, regardless of modality. A result favouring Baddeley would be strong interference between dissimilar contents that share a subsystem and little between similar contents in different subsystems; the reverse pattern would favour Cowan. Cowan's account also handles expertise effects naturally, since activated long-term knowledge is what working memory is made of.

Question 10

What makes the oculomotor delayed-response task a clean test of working memory? Refer to the fixation requirement in your answer.

Show the answerHide the answer

The monkey had to keep fixating a central spot throughout the delay, with nothing on the screen, and only after the fixation point went off could it look to where the cue had been. Because it could not move its eyes to the target during the delay, it could not solve the task by simply keeping its gaze, or a prepared eye position, on the cued location. Whatever bridged the gap had to be an internal representation of the location, so delay activity recorded during that interval reflects working memory.

Question 11

What is a memory field, and why did Funahashi and colleagues choose that name? Explain how the error-trial and delay-lengthening findings strengthen the claim that delay activity is the memory rather than an aftereffect of the cue.

Show the answerHide the answer

A memory field is the region of visual space for which a prefrontal neuron fires during the delay: the neuron fires tonically when the remembered cue was in a particular direction, and weakly or with suppression for the opposite direction. Funahashi and colleagues named it by analogy with the receptive field of a visual neuron and the movement field of an oculomotor neuron: a receptive field describes the space a neuron responds to, a memory field the space it remembers. When the delay was lengthened from three to six seconds, the firing expanded to fill it, so the activity tracks the memory requirement rather than persisting for a fixed time after the cue. On error trials, delay activity was truncated or absent, so when the firing failed the memory failed. That correspondence rested on only nine neurons, so it should be held tentatively.

Question 12

Recording, lesion and clinical evidence are said to converge on the prefrontal cortex. Explain what each kind of evidence contributes that the others cannot, and why backward digit span is more informative here than forward digit span.

Show the answerHide the answer

Recording shows what happens: prefrontal neurons hold a location during the delay, but this is correlational. Lesions show necessity: monkeys with prefrontal damage fail the delayed-response task, and unilateral damage produces errors for targets in the opposite visual field, matching the memory-field organisation. Clinical evidence shows the same pattern in humans: patients with prefrontal damage from stroke, tumour or aneurysm, and people with frontotemporal dementia, are impaired on demanding working-memory tasks. Backward digit span is more informative than forward span because it requires holding a sequence and manipulating it, not merely echoing it. Prefrontal patients often have normal forward span but impaired backward span, suggesting that what prefrontal damage costs is mainly the executive manipulation rather than storage, consistent with the sensory-recruitment view that content is stored posteriorly and prefrontal cortex supplies control.

Question 13

What is the contralateral delay activity, and why is it a more useful measure of the contents of working memory than a measure that tracked task difficulty?

Show the answerHide the answer

The contralateral delay activity (CDA) is a sustained negative voltage over the hemisphere opposite to the side of a display whose items are being held in visual working memory. It persists through the retention interval and its amplitude increases with the number of items held, levelling off at each person's capacity. A measure that tracked task difficulty would rise for any demanding condition and could not distinguish holding more items from simply working harder. Because the CDA tracks the number of objects stored, it gives a read-out of what is actually in memory, which is what made it possible to see whether irrelevant items had been stored.

Question 14

Explain how Vogel and colleagues' findings connect working-memory capacity to attention, and why the claim that low-capacity individuals may store more information is not a contradiction. What did the add-versus-exclude experiment rule out?

Show the answerHide the answer

Participants held two relevant items, four relevant items, or two relevant items with two irrelevant ones to ignore. High-capacity individuals showed a CDA like the two-item condition in the mixed display, so they excluded the irrelevant items; low-capacity individuals showed a CDA like the four-item condition, so they stored them too. Capacity and filtering efficiency were strongly correlated, linking working-memory capacity to an attentional skill. It is not a contradiction that low-capacity individuals may store more, because much of what they store is irrelevant: part of a large effective capacity is not admitting the wrong things. The add-versus-exclude experiment showed both groups added new items with perfect additivity, while only exclusion differed, ruling out the possibility that low-capacity individuals were simply poor at controlling working memory in general.

Question 15

In high-capacity participants, delay activity rose briefly towards the four-item level before falling back. Why does that time course complicate the description of filtering as a gate?

Show the answerHide the answer

A gate would block irrelevant items at the door, so delay activity should stay flat at the two-item level. Instead the CDA in high-capacity participants briefly rose towards the four-item level when the irrelevant items appeared and then dropped back. The irrelevant items were admitted and then ejected. What distinguishes good filterers is a rapid correction after the junk is already inside, not a barrier that keeps it out. This shifts the question from where the selection point sits, as in the early-versus-late debate, to how quickly the system can undo its own admissions.

Question 16

Panichello and Buschman found that a classifier trained on selection from memory could decode attention to stimuli in prefrontal cortex but not in parietal cortex or V4. What does that dissociation suggest about where cognitive control is implemented? Give one respect in which selection and attention were nonetheless shown to differ.

Show the answerHide the answer

In lateral prefrontal cortex, the code for selecting an item from memory generalised to the code for attending to a stimulus, and control information appeared there first before propagating backwards to parietal cortex and V4. In frontal eye fields, parietal cortex and V4 the two kinds of control were represented independently. This suggests prefrontal cortex acts as a domain-general controller issuing the same selection signal for memories and stimuli, while posterior regions keep separate codes. Selection and attention differed in competitive suppression: attention suppresses the representation of unattended neighbours, but selection from working memory did not reduce information about the unselected memory in prefrontal or parietal cortex. (Selection also re-formatted the chosen item into a shared subspace used to guide the response, and weaker alignment went with less accurate reports.)
