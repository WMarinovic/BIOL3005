Question 1

Explain how Donders used the difference between choice and simple reaction time to estimate the duration of a decision. What assumption does the subtractive method rely on?

Show the answerHide the answer

Donders measured simple reaction time (press one button when a light appears), go/no-go reaction time (respond to one designated stimulus and withhold the response to others) and choice reaction time (a different button for each light). Each task contains the operations of the one before plus one more: go/no-go adds identifying the stimulus, and choice adds selecting which response to make. Subtracting one mean reaction time from another therefore estimates the duration of the added operation. Choice minus simple gives the combined time to identify the stimulus and select a response, which Donders treated as the time to decide, about 100 ms; choice minus go/no-go isolates response selection alone. The method rests on the assumption of pure insertion: adding a stage leaves the other stages unchanged, so the components simply add up. If inserting an operation altered how the shared stages ran, the difference in reaction times would no longer measure the inserted stage alone.

Question 2

Why did introspection fail as a method, and how does that failure connect to the claim that most cognition is unconscious?

Show the answerHide the answer

Introspection asked trained observers to report the elements of their conscious experience. Even under Wundt's strict conditions (practised observers, repeatable stimuli) observers never reached agreement, and because the reports were subjective there was no way to check from outside whether anyone was describing their mental contents accurately. The deeper problem is that most cognition is unconscious. The regulation of breathing, the filtering of background sound and the holding of the previous sentence in mind all go on without awareness, so introspection can only ever reach the small fraction of processing that surfaces into consciousness. A method that can only see the rider will always have a low ceiling for explaining a mind that is mostly elephant.

Question 3

What is a double dissociation, and why is the Broca–Wernicke pairing stronger evidence for localisation than a single case of aphasia would be?

Show the answerHide the answer

A double dissociation is a pair of complementary findings: damage to one region impairs function A but spares function B, while damage to another region impairs B but spares A. Damage to Broca's area in the frontal lobe impairs speech production while leaving comprehension of everyday language largely intact; damage to Wernicke's area in the temporal lobe impairs comprehension while leaving speech fluent, though empty of meaning. A single case of aphasia shows only that one ability can be lost while another survives, which could happen simply because one task is harder or more fragile than the other. When each ability can be lost while the other is spared, that explanation fails: the two cannot be one function riding on a shared mechanism, so they are separable and depend on different regions.

Question 4

A split-brain patient cannot name an object flashed to the left visual field but can pick it out with the left hand. Explain why, in terms of hemispheric wiring and lateralisation.

Show the answerHide the answer

Each visual field projects to the opposite hemisphere, so an object flashed to the left visual field reaches only the right hemisphere. In most people language is lateralised to the left hemisphere. With the corpus callosum cut, the right hemisphere cannot pass the information across, so the verbal left hemisphere never receives it and the patient cannot name the object. The right hemisphere has nonetheless seen it, and because each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body, it can guide the left hand to pick the object out by touch. Flash the same object to the right visual field and it reaches the language-dominant left hemisphere, so the patient names it easily. In an intact brain the two hemispheres work together, so the split should not be overstated.

Question 5

Distinguish episodic, semantic, and procedural memory, and give an everyday example of each.

Show the answerHide the answer

Episodic memory is memory for personally experienced events, with their what, where and when, such as remembering your first lecture in this unit and where you sat. Semantic memory is factual knowledge detached from the occasion on which it was learned, such as knowing that Perth is the capital of Western Australia without remembering when you learned it. Both are explicit (declarative) because they can be consciously brought to mind. Procedural memory is implicit (non-declarative) memory for skilled actions, expressed in performance rather than recollection, such as riding a bike, swimming freestyle or typing without looking at the keys.

Question 6

Why does random assignment license a causal inference, and what is a confound? Explain how a double-blind procedure protects against two specific confounds.

Show the answerHide the answer

When participants are randomly assigned, they do not choose their condition, so on average the groups are equivalent on everything except the manipulated independent variable. Any reliable difference in the dependent variable can then be attributed to the manipulation, which is what licenses a causal conclusion. A confound is anything that varies alongside the independent variable, so that its effect cannot be separated from the effect of the manipulation. In a double-blind procedure neither the participant nor the experimenter knows which condition the participant is in. Keeping participants blind protects against placebo effects and participant demand, because their expectations no longer differ between conditions. Keeping experimenters blind protects against experimenter expectations, because they cannot treat the groups differently or score them with a bias, even unintentionally.

Question 7

Cherry found that separating two spoken messages was extremely difficult when both were recorded in the same voice on one tape, but easy when one message went to each ear. Explain what that contrast reveals about the basis on which attention selects, and how Broadbent's filter model accounted for it. What later finding forced the filter to be revised?

Show the answerHide the answer

The contrast shows that selection is easy when the messages arrive on physically distinguishable channels and very hard when they share one. With the same voice on one tape, the listener struggled, closed his eyes and replayed phrases many times, and relied on knowledge of which words are likely to follow which; when the messages were built from clichés with arbitrary junctions, they could not be separated at all. With one message to each ear, the listener could follow either at will. So attention appears to select on simple physical properties such as ear, pitch and voice rather than on meaning. Broadbent's filter model captured this: incoming messages are held briefly in a sensory store, a filter selects one on its physical characteristics, and only that message passes on for analysis of meaning, so selection is early. The model had to be revised when Treisman showed that listeners follow a meaningful passage when it switches to the rejected ear, and Moray showed that people notice their own name there. Unattended messages are therefore attenuated, turned down like a volume control, rather than blocked.

Question 8

Why should a computer program that proved theorems in logic have mattered to psychologists who study people? Refer in your answer to the behaviourist objection to talking about internal mental states.

Show the answerHide the answer

Behaviourists rejected talk of internal mental states such as goals, strategies or memory because these could not be observed and so seemed unscientific. The Logic Theorist was a physical system that manipulated symbols to prove theorems, working backwards from a goal by applying a small set of strategies and holding expressions in a limited working memory. It showed that internal states and processes, the very things behaviourism ruled out, could be specified precisely, implemented and tested. If a machine could properly be described in terms of goals, strategies and working memory, there was no principled reason to refuse that vocabulary when describing a person. Its designers also described it with a diagram of labelled stages and arrows, the ancestor of the box-and-arrow models of cognitive psychology.

Question 9

Distinguish specificity, population, and sparse coding. Why is strict specificity coding an implausible account of how you recognise a particular familiar face, and what does sparse coding offer instead?

Show the answerHide the answer

In specificity (labelled-line) coding, one neuron is tuned to one stimulus or quality, so its activity signals that quality, as with pain receptors. In population coding, each neuron responds to a range of values and the percept is carried by the pattern of firing across many neurons, as when colour is recovered from the combined responses of the three cone types. In sparse coding, a stimulus is represented by a small subset of neurons firing while the great majority stay silent. Strict specificity coding of a familiar face would need a dedicated cell for every recognisable person and object, and losing that cell would lose the concept, which is implausible. Sparse coding keeps the narrow selectivity that recordings in higher visual and medial temporal areas actually find, but spreads each representation over a small group of cells rather than one. Olshausen and Field argue that this increases associative-memory capacity, makes the structure of natural signals explicit, eases read-out at later stages and saves metabolic energy.

Question 10

Two brain regions show strongly correlated activity, but there is no direct axonal tract between them. Explain how this is possible using the distinction between structural and functional connectivity. Why does the existence of resting-state networks such as the default mode network complicate the assumption that cognition happens only when a task has been set?

Show the answerHide the answer

Structural connectivity refers to the physical wiring, the axonal tracts that anatomically link regions. Functional connectivity is a statistical relationship: two regions are functionally connected if their activity rises and falls together over time. The two need not coincide, because two regions can be coordinated by communicating through an intermediate hub, so correlated activity is evidence of coordination rather than of a direct cable. Resting-state networks complicate the idea that cognition happens only when a task is set, because even when a person lies in a scanner doing nothing in particular, activity organises itself into reproducible networks. The default mode network is most active precisely when attention is not directed outward and is associated with mind-wandering and self-referential thought, so a great deal of brain activity is not in the service of any task the experimenter has imposed.

Question 11

A study reports that two regions are active while participants perform a task. Explain why this does not show that either region is necessary for the task, and describe how TMS could be used to test both necessity and timing. Give two limitations of that approach.

Show the answerHide the answer

Imaging is correlational. Activity in a region during a task shows that the region is engaged at the time, but it may be responding to something incidental, or merely be along for the ride, so activation alone cannot show the region is necessary. TMS intervenes rather than observes: a brief pulse induces a current that temporarily disrupts a patch of cortex, a reversible virtual lesion in a healthy brain. If disrupting a region reliably impairs performance, the region was doing part of the work, as when occipital TMS disrupted Braille reading in early-blind readers but not in sighted controls. Because a single pulse acts only briefly, varying its timing relative to the stimulus also shows when a region contributes (causal chronometry); in a choice reaction-time task, pulses slowed responses at about 140 ms over anterior premotor cortex, 180 ms over posterior premotor cortex and from 220 ms over sensorimotor cortex. Limitations include coarse and uncertain spatial resolution, with effects possibly spreading to connected regions; access only to surface cortex, so structures such as the hippocampus and basal ganglia are out of reach; effects that can facilitate rather than disrupt; and a seizure risk with repetitive TMS, which requires safety screening and ethical approval.
