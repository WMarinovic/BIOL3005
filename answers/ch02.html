Chapter 2 answers

Attention

Model answers to the 16 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 2. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbookRead Chapter 2

Question 1

In a shadowing task, what can people report about the unattended message, and what can't they? What does this tell you about the limits of processing?

Show the answerHide the answer

People can shadow and report the attended message easily, but of the unattended message they can report only crude physical properties, such as whether the voice was male or female, or a change in pitch. They typically miss its meaning and even striking changes to it: a switch from English to German, or the same word repeated 35 times. This shows that the capacity to process meaning is tightly limited, so the system must select what it analyses in depth, and the question becomes where in the stream that selection happens.

Question 2

State the key problem with Broadbent's early filter, and explain how Treisman's attenuation model solves it.

Show the answerHide the answer

Broadbent's filter selects one channel on physical features and blocks the rest completely before meaning is analysed. The problem is that meaning from the unattended channel sometimes gets through: people hear their own name in the ignored ear, they follow meaning across ears (reporting "Dear Aunt Jane" from "Dear 7 Jane" and "9 Aunt 6"), and in Treisman's own studies they followed a story when it switched to the wrong ear. Treisman kept selection early but replaced the all-or-nothing gate with an attenuator. The unattended message is turned down rather than switched off, and whatever survives reaches a dictionary unit in which each word has its own recognition threshold. Important or frequent words, above all your own name, have low thresholds and can be recognised even from a weakened signal. Selection is early, but leaky.

Question 3

In MacKay's experiment, participants could not report the word played to the unattended ear, yet that word shifted how they interpreted the attended sentence. Explain why this result is fatal to a strictly early filter, and what Treisman's attenuator would say about it.

Show the answerHide the answer

The unattended word could not be reported, yet it biased the interpretation of the ambiguous attended sentence towards a riverbank or a financial bank. To do that, the word must have been processed all the way to its meaning. A strictly early filter blocks the unattended channel before any semantic analysis, so it predicts no such influence; the result is therefore fatal to it. Treisman's attenuator can accommodate it: the unattended word is weakened rather than blocked, and the attended sentence has primed the related meanings, lowering their thresholds in the dictionary unit, so the attenuated word can still activate its meaning and shape interpretation without reaching report. (MacKay's result is also exactly what Deutsch and Deutsch's late-selection model predicts.)

Question 4

Explain how load theory accounts for both early-selection-like and late-selection-like results with a single mechanism.

Show the answerHide the answer

Load theory holds that perceptual capacity is limited and is spent involuntarily on whatever is present. When the relevant task carries high perceptual load (many items or a demanding discrimination), the target uses up all the capacity and nothing is left for distractors, so they are not processed and selection looks early. When the task carries low perceptual load, the spare capacity spills over onto the distractors whether or not you want it to, so they are processed and intrude, and selection looks late. A single mechanism, the amount of capacity left over, therefore produces both patterns, with early and late selection as two ends of a continuum set by load.

Question 5

The cross task varies load while holding the display identical. Why does that design matter, and what alternative explanation does it rule out?

Show the answerHide the answer

In the cross task the display is identical in both conditions: a briefly shown cross with one green and one blue arm, one slightly longer. Under low load participants report which arm is green; under high load they report which arm is longer. Because the stimulation is the same and only the judgement differs, any difference in what else is noticed, such as an unexpected peripheral shape, cannot be due to differences in the display, for example more items, more clutter or different visual crowding. It isolates the demand placed on perceptual capacity as the cause.

Question 6

Macdonald and Lavie found that detection sensitivity (d′) for a peripheral shape fell under high perceptual load with no change in response criterion. Explain why the criterion result is essential to their interpretation.

Show the answerHide the answer

Detection sensitivity (d′) measures how well a person can tell stimulus-present from stimulus-absent trials, whereas the response criterion measures how willing they are to say "present". If high load had simply made participants more conservative, more reluctant to report the shape, detection rates would fall through a criterion shift, and the result could be explained by a decision or reporting bias rather than by perception. Because d′ fell while the criterion stayed the same, the drop reflects a genuine reduction in what was perceived. Combined with the design features that ruled out forgetting and neglect of the detection task, this is what licenses the conclusion that load reduces perception itself.

Question 7

Load reduces the V1 response to an image that observers never consciously see. What does that finding tell you about the relationship between attention and awareness?

Show the answerHide the answer

Using continuous flash suppression, the tool images were invisible under both low and high load, yet their V1 response still shrank under high load (Bahrami et al., 2007). The effect of attention therefore cannot depend on awareness, because it operates on stimuli that never reached awareness in either condition. Attention is not simply a gate on consciousness; it is a limit on processing, much of which was never going to become conscious anyway. Attention and awareness are therefore dissociable.

Question 8

Load theory distinguishes perceptual load from cognitive (working-memory) load, and the two make opposite predictions about distraction. State both predictions, and explain why they are not a contradiction.

Show the answerHide the answer

High perceptual load reduces distraction, because the relevant task consumes perceptual capacity and leaves none for distractors. High cognitive (working-memory) load increases distraction: when executive control processes are busy holding digits or tracking a changing sequence, they are less able to keep the task priorities straight, so irrelevant items win more often, as in the sandwich task results. The two are not contradictory because they tax different resources. Perceptual load acts on the capacity to perceive; cognitive load acts on the control that maintains what counts as relevant. Within working memory the distinction is finer still: maintenance of visual material behaves like perceptual load, while loading control increases awareness of distractors, especially when the distractor competes with the target.

Question 9

Summarise the dilution account of load effects and the reply to it. What kind of result would count as evidence against load theory, and why is that harder to specify than it looks?

Show the answerHide the answer

The dilution account (Tsal & Benoni, 2010) argues that many load manipulations add neutral items to the display, which raises load but also dilutes the distractor's influence because it now competes with several items. When they kept the number of items constant but made the target pop out (low load, high dilution), interference disappeared, so they attributed display-size effects to dilution rather than capacity. Lavie and Torralbo (2010) replied that spillover under low load still goes somewhere, in dilution displays to the neutral letters near the target, which then produced the response-competition effects, and that peripheral distractor effects return when spillover favours them. A common current position is a hybrid in which both effects reflect competition between stimuli for neural representation. Evidence against the theory would be distractor processing under genuinely high perceptual load. That is hard to specify because there is no measure of load independent of its effects: high load is defined as load sufficient to stop distractor processing, so interference under supposed high load can always be blamed on a weak manipulation. This circularity makes clean refutation difficult.

Question 10

Memory for a scene is better, not worse, when the scene coincides with a target in an unrelated detection task. Why is that awkward for the claim that perceptual capacity is spent obligatorily and completely?

Show the answerHide the answer

Load theory claims that capacity is always fully used, so whatever the target consumes is taken from everything else. Detecting the white target square demands attention, so on that account the scene shown at the same moment should be remembered worse. In the attentional boost effect it is remembered better, by roughly 10 to 20 percentage points, and the obvious alternatives (distinctiveness, arousal, spatial cueing) have been ruled out. If capacity is always in use, a transient boost has nothing to draw on; if capacity expands whenever something relevant happens, it becomes unclear what would count as evidence against the theory. Swallow and Jiang propose a brief enhancement selective in time but not in space or modality, plausibly driven by phasic noradrenaline release from the locus coeruleus.

Question 11

Distinguish overt from covert attention and endogenous from exogenous orienting. What is inhibition of return?

Show the answerHide the answer

Overt attention shifts by moving the eyes to a location; covert attention shifts without any eye movement, as when you watch someone out of the corner of your eye. Exogenous orienting is reflexive and stimulus-driven, triggered by a sudden peripheral event whether or not it is useful (the elephant reacting). Endogenous orienting is voluntary and goal-driven, triggered by a central symbolic cue such as an arrow that must be interpreted (the rider choosing). Inhibition of return is a signature of the reflexive system: an exogenous cue first speeds responses at its location, but from roughly 300 ms after the cue responses there become slower, as if attention is discouraged from returning to a place it has already visited.

Question 12

Distinguish location-based from object-based attention. What is the same-object advantage, and to which kind of orienting does attentional capture belong?

Show the answerHide the answer

Location-based attention selects a region of space, as in the Posner cueing paradigm. Object-based attention selects a perceptual object, so that attending to part of an object spreads the benefit across the whole of it. The same-object advantage is the finding that a target is detected faster when it appears elsewhere on the attended object than at an equally distant point on a different object (Duncan, 1984; Egly et al., 1994). Attentional capture, in which a salient, sudden or distinctive stimulus seizes attention automatically whether or not it is relevant, belongs to exogenous orienting.

Question 13

What is an illusory conjunction, and how does it support the two-stage claim of Feature Integration Theory? Predict the search slopes for feature versus conjunction search.

Show the answerHide the answer

An illusory conjunction is a wrong combination of correctly registered features, such as reporting a red triangle when the display held a red item and a triangle that were not one object. They occur when a display is flashed briefly and attention is loaded elsewhere, and disappear when observers have time to attend to each location. This supports Feature Integration Theory's two stages: features are registered separately and in parallel before attention (so the colour and the shape are both detected), and focused attention is needed to bind them at a location (so without it they recombine at random). For search, a target defined by a single feature pops out, so search time stays roughly flat as set size increases. A conjunction target requires binding at each location in turn, so search time rises steeply, roughly linearly, with set size.

Question 14

Why does a hands-free phone conversation impair driving as much as a hand-held one? Frame your answer in terms of capacity and inattentional blindness.

Show the answerHide the answer

The main danger of phone use while driving is cognitive rather than manual. A conversation consumes the limited attentional capacity that noticing hazards requires, whether or not the driver's hands are free, and it does so far more than listening to the radio, because conversation demands active engagement. With capacity consumed, drivers show inattentional blindness: they fail to register events they have looked straight at. Removing the handset does nothing to restore that capacity, so hands-free and hand-held calls impair driving to a similar degree. Only around two and a half per cent of people seem able to drive unimpaired while on a phone (Watson & Strayer, 2010).

Question 15

Under a consistent mapping, extensive practice turns search into an automatic process; under a varied mapping it does not. Explain why the mapping matters, and name one cost of a process having become automatic.

Show the answerHide the answer

Under a consistent mapping a given item is always a target and never a distractor, so practice builds a stable association between the item and the response, and detection can become automatic: fast, effortless, largely unaffected by the number of distractors, and hard to switch off. Under a varied mapping today's target is tomorrow's distractor, so no stable association can form and search stays controlled, slow and serial. One cost of automaticity is that the process is hard to suppress when it is unwanted. Another is that attending to an over-learned skill can disrupt it, which is what happens when people choke under pressure.

Question 16

Give one example of attending to something you are not conscious of, and one of being conscious of something you are barely attending to. What does the pair show about the relationship between attention and awareness?

Show the answerHide the answer

Attending without awareness: under continuous flash suppression, attentional load still reduces the V1 response to tool images that the observer never sees, and a masked number can guide a response without being consciously seen. Awareness with little attention: you can be aware of the general hum of a room, or that someone is standing at the edge of your vision, while your attention is on a conversation. The pair shows that attention and awareness are distinct. Attention is a limit on processing that can operate below awareness, while awareness depends on further conditions, plausibly reciprocal, synchronised exchange of signals across brain regions. We need attention for a great deal, but conscious awareness for less than we assume.
