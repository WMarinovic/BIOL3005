Chapter 6 answers

Everyday Memory and Memory Errors

Model answers to the 19 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 6. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbookRead Chapter 6

Question 1

What makes autobiographical memory multidimensional, and why is the own-photographs versus laboratory-photographs comparison a better test of self-relevance than simply comparing vivid with dull memories?

Show the answerHide the answer

Recalling a personal event recruits a broad network of brain regions rather than a single store, because the original experience bundled together vision, sound, emotion, place and self, and retrieval has to reassemble that bundle. Comparing vivid with dull memories confounds self-relevance with many other differences, such as emotional intensity, distinctiveness, rehearsal and content. In Cabeza and colleagues' study, people's own photographs and someone else's photographs showed the same locations, so what was depicted was held constant and the main difference was whether the memory was the person's own. Their own photographs produced stronger medial prefrontal and hippocampal activity, as well as stronger visual and parahippocampal activity, isolating the effect of self-relevance.

Question 2

Why does the loss of autobiographical memory in dementia amount to more than the loss of information? Refer to §4.4 in your answer.

Show the answerHide the answer

Section 4.4 argued that episodic memory is what places a person at particular moments in their own past, which is why Tulving called its awareness self-knowing: the self that persists through time depends on the system that remembers past events. Autobiographical memory supplies the narrative a person uses to know who they are. In dementia, particularly Alzheimer's disease, its progressive loss therefore strips away not just stored facts but the scaffolding of identity, the sense of being a continuous someone with a history.

Question 3

The reminiscence bump shifts to a later age in people who emigrate in adulthood. Which of the three hypotheses does this support, and why is a prediction that could have failed better evidence than one that could not?

Show the answerHide the answer

It supports the cognitive hypothesis, which says encoding is best during periods of rapid change followed by stability, when experiences are novel and distinctive. In people who emigrate in adulthood, the bump moves to the age at immigration, when the flood of novelty occurred, although the cultural life script did not change and identity formation had largely run its course. The hypothesis made a risky prediction: if the bump had stayed in adolescence and early adulthood, the hypothesis would have been in trouble. A prediction that could have failed and did not is stronger evidence than one compatible with any outcome, because only the former genuinely tests the theory.

Question 4

Why might the reminiscence bump be overdetermined? Explain how all three hypotheses could be partly correct at once.

Show the answerHide the answer

Overdetermined means that several causes each push towards the same outcome, so the effect would appear even if one cause were removed. Adolescence and early adulthood are periods of rapid change and novelty (cognitive hypothesis), the period when a stable self-image forms, so self-defining events are encoded well and rehearsed (self-image hypothesis), and the period containing most culturally scripted milestones such as leaving home and a first job, which give retrieval a ready-made structure (life-script hypothesis). The hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and each has distinctive support: the bump shifts with emigration, as the cognitive account predicts, but it appears for happy and important memories and not for sad or traumatic ones, as the life-script account predicts. All three can be partly correct at once.

Question 5

A friend insists their memory of hearing shocking news is burned in and therefore accurate. Using Talarico and Rubin (2003), explain why their confidence is no guarantee of accuracy, and say why the everyday-memory comparison condition was essential to the argument.

Show the answerHide the answer

Talarico and Rubin recorded students' memories of hearing about the September 11 attacks the day after the event, together with an ordinary event from the same period, and retested each person once, 1, 6 or 32 weeks later. Consistency with the original report declined at much the same rate for the flashbulb memory as for the everyday memory. Confidence, however, stayed high for the flashbulb memory while it fell for the everyday one. So the friend's certainty reflects how vivid the memory feels, not how accurate it is. The everyday-memory condition was essential because it gave a matched comparison: both memories were recorded at the same time and tested on the same schedule, so the flashbulb memory could be compared against an ordinary memory rather than against intuition, revealing that only confidence differed.

Question 6

The amygdala stamps in emotional memories and each retelling rehearses them. Explain why these two mechanisms do not make flashbulb memories more accurate, referring to reconsolidation in §5.8.

Show the answerHide the answer

The amygdala modulates how strongly emotional memories are consolidated, stamping in the surrounding detail, and narrative rehearsal consolidates the memory further with each retelling. Both make the memory stronger and more vivid, but neither checks that what is being stamped in or rehearsed is accurate. Section 5.8 adds that each retrieval may reopen the memory and return it to a labile state, so that what is re-stored is the version just told, including any distortions. Rehearsal could therefore make the memory more confident and more consistent with the latest retelling while carrying it further from the original event. This is a plausible mechanism rather than a demonstrated one, but it fits Talarico and Rubin's results.

Question 7

What is a schema, and what did Bartlett's War of the Ghosts demonstrate about how schemas act on memory over repeated retellings?

Show the answerHide the answer

A schema is an organised packet of knowledge about how some part of the world usually works; a script is a schema for a sequence of events, such as eating at a restaurant. Bartlett had British participants repeatedly recall an unfamiliar Native American folk tale. With each retelling the story drifted towards their own cultural expectations: canoes became boats, and unfamiliar supernatural elements were dropped or rationalised into something more familiar. Schemas therefore act on memory over time, filling gaps with what was expected rather than what was experienced, so people remember the story their schema leads them to expect. The same structure that makes encoding efficient also quietly reshapes memory.

Question 8

Studying a list of related words often produces confident recognition of a related word that was never presented. Explain why this is a by-product of successful encoding rather than a failure of attention.

Show the answerHide the answer

When you study a list of related words, you extract its gist, its shared meaning, rather than storing it as a string of unrelated sounds. A closely related word that was never presented fits that gist so well that it is generated from it, and the resulting sense of having seen it is indistinguishable from the sense of having seen the real items. The false memory is produced by exactly the meaning-extracting machinery that makes encoding successful. It is not a lapse of attention or a guess under pressure.

Question 9

Distinguish a pragmatic inference from a source-monitoring error, and give an example of each.

Show the answerHide the answer

A pragmatic inference is going beyond what was said to what was implied, then remembering the inference as if it had been stated. Told that the karate champion hit the cinder block, people later recall that he broke it; told that the baby stayed awake all night, they may recall that it cried all night. A source-monitoring error is misattributing where a memory came from while its content is intact. Examples are judging a non-famous name famous a day after reading it in a list, or remembering a claim but forgetting that it came from an unreliable website. The first adds content that was never presented; the second keeps the content and loses its origin.

Question 10

Explain how a source-monitoring failure underlies both the becoming-famous-overnight result and the misidentification of an innocent bystander in a lineup. What is preserved in each case, and what is lost?

Show the answerHide the answer

In the becoming-famous-overnight study, reading a non-famous name gave it familiarity that survived a day's delay, while memory for the source ("I saw it in yesterday's list") faded. Left with unexplained familiarity, participants attributed it to the most plausible source: the name must be famous. In a lineup, an innocent bystander who was present at the scene has a familiar face, and if the witness has lost the source of that familiarity (seeing him reading to students, for example), it can be misattributed to his having been the perpetrator. In both cases familiarity is preserved and its label, where the familiarity came from, is lost. Source monitoring fails not by losing the memory but by losing its label.

Question 11

Why does the illusory-truth effect mean that a false claim need not defeat your reasoning in order to influence you? Relate your answer to fluency and to the Rider and the Elephant.

Show the answerHide the answer

Repeating a claim makes it easier to process, and that fluency is misread as a sign of truth. The effect works through how the claim feels rather than through argument, so a false claim does not have to win a debate with your reasoning; it only has to become familiar. In the book's terms, the Elephant generates the feeling of truth automatically and the Rider receives it as if it were its own judgement, with nothing to inspect or rebut. That is why the effect survives an explicit warning that the material may be false.

Question 12

Lindsay (1990) found the misinformation effect was largest when the misleading source was hard to distinguish from the original. Why does that result place the misinformation effect inside source monitoring rather than alongside it?

Show the answerHide the answer

Lindsay varied how easily the misleading information could be distinguished from the original event, in timing and presentation conditions, and told participants that nothing in the misleading narrative was correct, so any suggested detail they reported must have been misattributed to the event. When the sources were hard to tell apart, the suggested detail was reported on 27 per cent of misled items against 9 per cent of controls; when the misleading information came two days later under clearly different conditions, the difference nearly vanished (13 against 10 per cent), even though that group had heard the misinformation most recently. If misinformation simply overwrote memory, the most recent exposure should have done the most damage. Instead the damage depended on whether people could tell where a detail came from, which shows the misinformation effect is a source-monitoring failure, not a separate phenomenon alongside it.

Question 13

Ross and colleagues found 60 per cent misidentification of the bystander among witnesses who had seen him, and 18 per cent among those who had not. Explain why the comparison, rather than the 60 per cent alone, is what shows familiarity is doing the work.

Show the answerHide the answer

Sixty per cent on its own could reflect many things: the bystander might simply resemble the culprit, the lineup might be unfair, or witnesses might pick someone whenever the culprit is absent. The control group faced the same lineup, with the same faces and the true robber absent, but had never seen the innocent man. Their 18 per cent rate shows how often he would be chosen for those other reasons. The only difference between the groups was prior exposure to his face in an innocent setting, and that more than tripled the rate. The comparison isolates familiarity as the cause.

Question 14

A lineup is run by an officer who knows which person is the suspect, with no warning that the culprit may be absent. Identify two things wrong with this procedure and the specific error each invites.

Show the answerHide the answer

First, the administrator knows who the suspect is. Even unintentionally, they can steer the witness through cues and, after an identification, give confirming feedback, which inflates the witness's confidence and their reports of how good their view was (the post-identification feedback effect). A blind or double-blind administrator prevents this. Second, the witness is not warned that the culprit may be absent. Without that warning, witnesses treat the lineup as multiple choice and pick whoever best matches their memory, so when the culprit is absent an innocent person, especially a familiar one, is likely to be chosen.

Question 15

Why is a witness's confidence in court a poor guide to accuracy, and what would recording confidence at the moment of identification achieve?

Show the answerHide the answer

Confidence expressed in court is measured long after the identification and can have been inflated in the meantime, especially by confirming feedback such as "Good, you identified the suspect", which raises confidence without changing accuracy (Wells & Bradfield, 1998). Repeated questioning and rehearsal can raise it further. So a witness may be confident precisely because someone told them they were right. Recording confidence at the moment of identification, before anyone reacts, captures a judgement uncontaminated by later feedback. It provides a baseline against which later expressions of certainty can be compared, and a discrepancy between initial and courtroom confidence is itself informative.

Question 16

Kaesler and colleagues (2020) found sequential lineups change response bias but not discriminability. Explain the distinction, why a reduction in false identifications is not by itself evidence of improvement, and why this matters for policy.

Show the answerHide the answer

In signal-detection terms, discriminability is the witness's actual ability to tell a guilty face from an innocent one; response bias is how willing they are to choose anyone at all. Kaesler and colleagues found that sequential lineups make witnesses more conservative about choosing but leave discriminability little changed, and other analyses find a modest discriminability advantage for simultaneous lineups. A procedure that makes witnesses choose less often reduces false identifications, but it also reduces correct identifications, because both follow from the same reluctance to commit, so fewer false identifications alone is not an improvement. Only an increase in discriminability means witnesses are distinguishing better. This matters for policy because the sequential reform was adopted in many jurisdictions on the basis of reduced false identifications, a result that reflected a shift in bias rather than better memory.

Question 17

Set out the stages an intention passes through, and say which stage typically fails for a time-based intention and why.

Show the answerHide the answer

An intention is encoded together with the action it requires; held across a retention interval while you do other things; then, in the performance interval, you must notice that the right moment has arrived; you initiate and execute the action; and finally you evaluate whether it went to plan. For a time-based intention, the stage that typically fails is noticing the right moment in the performance interval. Nothing in the environment announces that it is 8 pm, so you must monitor the time yourself while busy with other things, and self-initiated monitoring is easily lost.

Question 18

Convert the time-based intention “phone the clinic at 3 pm” into an event-based one, and explain in terms of cues why the change should help.

Show the answerHide the answer

Make the call triggered by an event you know will occur around 3 pm: for example, "when I get back to my desk after the 2.30 lecture, I will phone the clinic", ideally with the clinic's number left on the keyboard, or set an alarm for 3 pm. An event-based intention is cued by something in the world, so the environment does part of the remembering: the sight of the desk, or the alarm, retrieves the intention. This removes the need for self-initiated monitoring of the clock, which is the stage at which time-based intentions usually fail.

Question 19

Explain how algorithmic content selection would be expected to interact with the illusory-truth effect, and why that interaction offers an account of political polarisation that does not require anyone to be irrational.

Show the answerHide the answer

An engagement-optimising algorithm shows you more of what you already respond to, so the claims you see repeatedly are not random but those you were already disposed to accept. Each repetition increases their fluency and therefore their felt truth through the illusory-truth effect, while source monitoring lets you remember the claim long after forgetting it came from an unreliable feed. If different communities are fed different repeated claims, each develops its own set of things that feel obviously true. Disagreement between groups then cannot be resolved by argument, because the beliefs were never established by argument in the first place. Polarisation can arise this way through ordinary memory mechanisms working as designed, without anyone being stupid, dishonest or irrational.
