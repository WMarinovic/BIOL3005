Chapter 9 answers

Language

Model answers to the 19 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 9. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbookRead Chapter 9

Question 1

Explain what is meant by saying language is hierarchical, rule-governed and arbitrary, giving an example of each.

Show the answerHide the answer

Hierarchical: a small set of elements (sounds, then words) combines without limit into larger units (phrases, then sentences), so a finite vocabulary yields an unbounded number of sentences; for example, "the cat" can be embedded in "the cat that the dog chased", and so on without end. Rule-governed: combinations follow a grammar, so "the cat chased the dog" means something different from "the dog chased the cat", and "chased dog the cat the" means nothing. Arbitrary: apart from a few onomatopoeic words, a word bears no resemblance to its referent; there is nothing dog-like about the sound "dog", and a community of speakers simply agrees on it.

Question 2

What observations about children's language did Chomsky use against Skinner, and what did he conclude? What did the debate not settle?

Show the answerHide the answer

Chomsky observed that children produce sentences they have never heard, and that were therefore never reinforced, and that they acquire complex grammatical rules from input too impoverished to specify those rules (the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument). (Children also make grammatical errors that were never reinforced.) He concluded that the core of language is substantially innate. The critique was decisive against a pure reinforcement account and reoriented the field towards the mental machinery of language. It did not settle how much of that machinery is language-specific rather than general-purpose learning, a question that statistical-learning findings have reopened.

Question 3

How does statistical learning in infants bear on the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument?

Show the answerHide the answer

Saffran and colleagues showed that eight-month-olds who heard two minutes of a continuous stream of nonsense syllables could later distinguish its "words" (syllables that reliably co-occurred) from sequences spanning word boundaries. Infants track transitional probabilities and use dips in predictability as cues to boundaries. This bears on the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument because it shows the input is richer than it looks once its statistics are counted, and that infants extract more structure from it, quickly, than was assumed. Whether statistical learning can reach the grammatical knowledge at the heart of the argument is still debated, so it weakens rather than refutes it.

Question 4

What is the word-frequency effect, and how do eye-movement data demonstrate it?

Show the answerHide the answer

The word-frequency effect is that people respond faster to common words than to rare ones; for example, they name pictured objects faster when the names are common (Wingfield, 1968). Eye-tracking during reading shows it directly: readers fixate low-frequency words for longer than high-frequency words (Rayner & Duffy, 1986). Fixation duration gives a moment-by-moment measure of processing during ordinary reading, without asking the reader to make any judgement.

Question 5

Why is variable pronunciation a problem for any account of word recognition based on matching the signal to a stored template?

Show the answerHide the answer

In running speech, words do not appear as clean instances of stored forms: "did you" surfaces as "did-joo" and "going to" as "gonna", and pronunciation varies across speakers, accents and contexts. A template-matching account needs the incoming signal to match a stored form; because the signal often does not, recognition must rely on knowledge brought to the signal, such as context and expectations about what is likely to be said, rather than on matching alone.

Question 6

On first meeting an ambiguous word, do we access one meaning or several? Cite the evidence, and explain the role of meaning dominance.

Show the answerHide the answer

We briefly access several. Tanenhaus and colleagues (1979) found that on first encountering an ambiguous word, people activate all its meanings, shown by the word priming responses to words related to any of its senses; only afterwards does context prune away the irrelevant meaning. Which meaning wins, and how fast, depends on meaning dominance. With biased dominance, one meaning is far more frequent and is reached quickly unless context forces the rarer one. With balanced dominance, the meanings are about equally frequent, so without context the reader is briefly slowed while they compete. Meaning resolution is a race between frequency and context.

Question 7

Explain why “While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods” is a garden-path sentence, and what late closure contributes to the explanation.

Show the answerHide the answer

Parsing is incremental, so we group words into phrases as they arrive. On reaching "the deer" we attach it as the object of "hunted" (the man hunted the deer). At "ran" that parse fails, because "ran" needs a subject, and we must backtrack and reinterpret "the deer" as the subject of a new clause ("the deer ran into the woods"). The sentence is only temporarily ambiguous, and the measurable slowdown at "ran" is the cost of reanalysis. Late closure is the heuristic of attaching each new word to the phrase currently being built, which is why "the deer" is bundled into the hunting clause in the first place, leading the reader up the garden path.

Question 8

How did Tanenhaus and colleagues (1995) use the visual world to distinguish the garden-path model from the constraint-based approach? Why is the timing of the context effect, rather than its existence, what settles the question?

Show the answerHide the answer

Listeners followed instructions such as "Put the apple on the towel in the box" while looking at a real scene. With one apple in the scene, they briefly looked at an empty towel, having parsed "on the towel" as the destination. With two apples, one on a towel, they did not, because they used the scene to parse "on the towel" as specifying which apple. Both models agree that context eventually influences interpretation. The garden-path model says syntax alone guides the initial parse and context only repairs it afterwards; the constraint-based approach says all information, including non-linguistic context, guides the parse immediately. The eye movements showed context shaping the parse within a fraction of a second, which is immediate guidance, so the timing rather than the mere existence of a context effect distinguishes the two.

Question 9

What is a situation model, and why is it, rather than the exact sentences, what we tend to remember?

Show the answerHide the answer

A situation model is a mental representation of the state of affairs a text describes, rather than of its words, built from the protagonist's point of view and filled in by inference and schemas. Readers represent the story almost as if experiencing the situation themselves. It is what survives because comprehension aims at what the text is about: the exact wording is discarded once the meaning is extracted and integrated, while the situation model is connected to existing knowledge and so is more durable.

Question 10

Explain why two readers with different cultural backgrounds can build different situation models from the same text without either misreading it.

Show the answerHide the answer

Building a situation model depends on inference, and inference draws on the schemas a reader actually has. A text that assumes knowledge one reader shares and another does not will support inferences for the first but not the second, and each reader imports their own schemas, filling gaps with what would be normal in their own world. Both can read the words accurately, and even recall them accurately, while building different models of the situation. This is a difference in background knowledge, not a failure of language or intelligence, which is why a comprehension test is partly a test of shared background.

Question 11

Explain syntactic priming, and why the fact that the second picture is different from the first matters to the interpretation.

Show the answerHide the answer

Syntactic priming (syntactic coordination) is the tendency to reuse a grammatical construction just produced or heard. Branigan and colleagues had a confederate describe a card with one of two grammatical forms, after which participants described a new card and tended to reuse the structure they had heard. It matters that the second picture was different because, if it had been the same, participants could have repeated the confederate's words or content. Reuse of the structure with different content shows that the grammatical form itself was primed, independently of meaning and words, which reduces the computational load of planning the next sentence.

Question 12

State the double dissociation between Broca's and Wernicke's aphasia, and give two reasons the classical two-area picture is a simplification.

Show the answerHide the answer

Broca's aphasia, following damage to Broca's area in the left frontal lobe, leaves speech laboured and agrammatical while comprehension is relatively intact (except for syntactically complex sentences). Wernicke's aphasia, following damage to Wernicke's area in the left temporal lobe, leaves speech fluent but empty while comprehension is poor. Production and comprehension are therefore separable. It is a simplification because, first, language depends on a distributed network including the connections between these regions, and real aphasias rarely fall neatly into two types; and second, the mapping to the left hemisphere, while typical, is not universal.

Question 13

Why is it a consequential error, rather than merely a discourtesy, to treat Aboriginal English as incorrect English? Set out the clinical consequence.

Show the answerHide the answer

Aboriginal English is a distinct, rule-governed dialect with its own systematic grammar, so a speaker is following rules rather than failing to follow Standard Australian English ones. Treating it as incorrect is a measurement error with real consequences. A standardised language assessment written for Standard Australian English counts dialect features as errors and can score a fluent Aboriginal English speaker as impaired. Children can then be referred for clinical intervention they do not need, while genuine language difficulties go unnoticed because the assessment is not measuring what it claims to measure.

Question 14

Name four dimensions along which bilingual speakers differ, and explain why a study recruiting by a single yes-or-no question may reach a different conclusion from another doing the same.

Show the answerHide the answer

Bilingual speakers differ in the age and context at which they acquired each language; in proficiency in each; in how often and in which settings they use each; in how similar their languages are; in the relative social prestige of the languages; and in their emotional relationship to each. (Any four will do.) A single yes-or-no question collapses all of this into a binary, so two studies using the same label may recruit very different people: one may get highly proficient speakers who switch frequently, the other people with rarely used schoolroom French. If an effect depends on particular aspects of bilingual experience, the studies will disagree for that reason alone.

Question 15

Distinguish simultaneous from sequential bilingual acquisition, and say what different problem each route faces.

Show the answerHide the answer

Simultaneous bilinguals acquire two languages from the start; sequential bilinguals add a second language after a first is established. A simultaneous learner must sort two systems apart while building both at once. A sequential learner already has one system in place, which can help (transferring knowledge and skills) but can also interfere with the new language.

Question 16

Why was codeswitching once read as confusion, and what is the evidence that it is instead a marker of competence? What does it have in common with the Aboriginal English case?

Show the answerHide the answer

Moving between languages was taken as a sign that a speaker lacked full mastery of either language and was mixing them because they could not keep them apart. The evidence now shows codeswitching is rule-governed: it tends to occur at particular syntactic boundaries, and doing it well requires fluency in both languages (Yow et al., 2018). Like Aboriginal English, it is a case where a deficit reading was imposed on what is really a competence: systematic, rule-following language use was mistaken for error because it was judged against a monolingual standard.

Question 17

The bilingual executive-function advantage replicates inconsistently. Give the most promising explanation for the inconsistency, and explain why a larger sample would not resolve it.

Show the answerHide the answer

The most promising explanation is that bilingualism is a graded, multidimensional profile rather than a trait, while studies typically recruit by a yes-or-no question. If only some profiles (high proficiency, frequent switching, immersion in a community using both languages) are associated with better executive control, studies that happen to recruit those people will find an effect and those that recruit others will not, even if every study is competently run and honestly reported. A larger sample would only estimate the average of that heterogeneous mixture more precisely; it would not separate the profiles that matter from those that do not. The remedy is to measure the dimensions of bilingual experience and model them.

Question 18

A meta-analysis finds that second-language learners achieve better mathematics results. Give one mechanistic explanation and one confound, and say what evidence would distinguish them.

Show the answerHide the answer

Mechanism: mathematics draws on executive abilities such as working memory, inhibition and flexibility, which managing and switching between two languages is thought to train. Confound: students who take up a second language are not a random sample. They may differ in prior ability, motivation, family socioeconomic background or school resources, any of which could produce both language study and better mathematics results. To distinguish them you would need designs that control selection: random assignment to language learning, or longitudinal studies measuring mathematics before and after starting a language and controlling for prior attainment and background, and ideally evidence that gains are mediated by improvements in executive function.

Question 19

Explain, using processing fluency, why a foreign accent can reduce the credibility of a claim, and why brief exposure to the accent reduces that effect. What does this imply about how to counter the bias?

Show the answerHide the answer

Foreign-accented speech is harder to process, harder processing feels like doubt, and that doubt is attributed to the claim rather than to the accent, so the claim seems less credible, the same fluency mechanism that drives the illusory-truth effect. Brief exposure to the accent makes it easier to process, which reduces the disfluency and so reduces the credibility penalty (Boduch-Grabka & Lev-Ari, 2021), as the fluency account predicts. (The original effect has a mixed replication record.) Because the bias arises automatically in processing, below deliberate judgement, it is not reliably corrected by trying to be fair. It is better countered by changing the conditions: increasing exposure to different accents, using structured assessment, and not relying on impressions of credibility where a decision matters.
