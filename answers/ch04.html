Chapter 4 answers

Long-Term Memory: Structure

Model answers to the 26 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 4. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbookRead Chapter 4

Question 1

Why does a filled delay after a word list abolish the recency effect but leave the primacy effect intact? What does that selective pattern tell you that the shape of the curve alone does not?

Show the answerHide the answer

A filled delay, such as thirty seconds of counting aloud, prevents rehearsal and empties the fragile short-term store, so the last few items, which were still being held there at the end of the list, are lost and recency disappears. The early items were rehearsed more and encoded into long-term memory, which the delay cannot reach, so primacy survives. The curve alone is only a shape that a single-store account could in principle produce. A manipulation that affects one end and not the other turns the two-store interpretation into evidence for two systems.

Question 2

Explain why long-term recency effects are awkward for the standard two-store reading of the serial position curve. Does the Glanzer and Cunitz result survive the objection?

Show the answerHide the answer

Recency advantages also appear over intervals far too long for any short-term store: people recall the most recent rugby matches they attended over several seasons, or the most recent restaurants they visited over months, most readily. So a recency advantage on its own cannot prove that a short-term store is responsible, and some theorists argue both ends of the curve reflect one retrieval process working on different degrees of distinctiveness. The Glanzer and Cunitz result survives the objection, because the selective effect of the filled delay, abolishing recency while sparing primacy, still needs explaining. "Recency equals short-term memory" is a serviceable rule rather than a law.

Question 3

What is release from proactive interference, and why does it imply that short-term memory can code by meaning? Identify precisely what changed and what did not at the point of release.

Show the answerHide the answer

Proactive interference is the build-up of old learning that impairs new learning. Over successive trials of three words from one category (fruits), each followed by a few seconds of counting backwards, recall falls as earlier items intrude. When the category switches (to professions), recall jumps back to about its original level: release from proactive interference. At the switch, everything physical stayed the same (number of words, presentation, test), and only the meaning of the items changed. The interfering system must therefore be organised by meaning, and because recall came only seconds after presentation, that semantic code is operating in short-term memory.

Question 4

Distinguish recall from recognition. Why should you be cautious about concluding from a recall failure that material has been forgotten?

Show the answerHide the answer

In recall you must generate the material yourself, either with no support (free recall) or with a hint such as a category name (cued recall). In recognition the item is supplied and you only decide whether you have met it before, which requires a decision rather than a search. Performance differs greatly between them: from fifty categorised words people might produce about a third in free recall, but around three quarters when cued with the categories, even though nothing was added to memory in between. A recall failure therefore shows only that the material could not be retrieved under those conditions, not that it has gone. It may be available but not accessible.

Question 5

What are remember and know judgements, and how do they connect to Tulving's distinction between autonoetic and noetic awareness?

Show the answerHide the answer

When people recognise an item, they can say whether they remember it, meaning they recollect the encounter with some of its context returning, or merely know it was there without any sense of the episode. The same correct recognition response can rest on either. Tulving distinguished autonoetic awareness, the self-knowing of episodic retrieval that amounts to mental time travel, from noetic awareness, knowing that something is so without reliving it. Remember judgements are the laboratory measure of autonoetic awareness, and know judgements of noetic awareness, which makes Tulving's distinction measurable in ordinary participants.

Question 6

Explain why H.M. and K.F. together provide stronger evidence than either alone. Define double dissociation in your answer, and state one thing a double dissociation does not establish.

Show the answerHide the answer

A double dissociation is a crossed pair: damage in one case impairs ability A and spares B, while damage in another impairs B and spares A. H.M. had normal short-term memory but could not form new long-term explicit memories; K.F. had a digit span of one or two items but formed long-term memories more or less normally. If memory were one store damaged to different degrees, whoever was worse off would be worse at everything; no amount of damage to one store produces both profiles, so the crossing establishes at least partly separate machinery. A double dissociation does not establish that the systems are wholly independent, or that each maps onto one tidy brain region.

Question 7

What does Clive Wearing's case add to H.M.'s, given that the two profiles are so similar?

Show the answerHide the answer

Clive Wearing reached a similar anterograde profile by a different cause, a brain infection rather than surgery, which is a converging case: it reduces the risk that something idiosyncratic about H.M., his surgery or history, explains the pattern. His case also extends the picture. His retrograde amnesia is far denser than H.M.'s, his semantic as well as episodic memory is impaired, and he cannot hold new experiences for more than about thirty seconds, yet he can still conduct and play the piano. That shows implicit procedural skill surviving even when explicit memory is very severely damaged.

Question 8

Distinguish episodic from semantic memory in terms of content, specificity, and the kind of awareness involved. Give an example of a dissociation between them.

Show the answerHide the answer

Content: episodic memory holds personally experienced events, their what, where and when; semantic memory holds facts and concepts about the world. Specificity: an episodic memory is of one particular occasion; a semantic memory is general knowledge that holds regardless of when it was learned. Awareness: episodic retrieval is autonoetic, re-experiencing the past from the inside (mental time travel); semantic retrieval is noetic, knowing without reliving. Dissociation: K.C., after hippocampal and medial temporal damage, could not recall any personally experienced event but kept his semantic knowledge, while L.P., with lateral temporal damage, showed impaired semantic memory with relatively spared episodic memory.

Question 9

K.C. and L.P. show opposite profiles. Set out the dissociation and explain why the fMRI finding of overlapping retrieval networks is consistent with it rather than a problem for it.

Show the answerHide the answer

K.C. lost episodic memory after hippocampal and medial temporal damage while semantic knowledge stayed largely intact; L.P., with lateral temporal cortex damage, lost semantic memory while episodic memory was relatively spared. Because each ability is lost in one patient and spared in the other, they form a double dissociation. fMRI in healthy people shows episodic retrieval leaning on hippocampus, medial temporal lobe and midline and parietal areas, and semantic retrieval on lateral temporal and frontal cortex, with substantial overlap. This is consistent with the patients because a double dissociation shows only that the systems are separable, not that they are anatomically independent. Separable systems sharing some machinery fit the patient data better than a clean split would.

Question 10

What is semanticisation, and how does it change the character of an autobiographical memory over time? Which component is lost and which is kept?

Show the answerHide the answer

Semanticisation is the gradual conversion of an episodic memory into semantic knowledge. An item often begins as an episode, the afternoon you learned that Canberra is the capital, and over time the episode dissolves and only the fact remains. Very old autobiographical memories therefore come to feel more like things known than events re-lived. What is lost is the autonoetic, re-experiencing component, the sense of being back in the scene; what is kept is the content.

Question 11

Why is episodic memory, rather than semantic memory, the one tied to a sense of self persisting through time? Use K.C. to explain what is lost and what survives, and distinguish personal semantic memory from episodic memory in your answer.

Show the answerHide the answer

Semantic memory gives you a world: a store of what is the case, which would be much the same whoever had acquired it. Every episodic memory has you in it, at a particular time, which is why Tulving called its awareness self-knowing. After his injury K.C. could not recall any personal event and, asked about his personal future as well as his past, described his mind as blank: he could not place himself at any moment other than the present. Yet he knew a great deal about his own life as fact. That surviving knowledge is personal semantic memory, general facts whose subject happens to be you, such as your date of birth. What he lost was episodic memory, the ability to re-enter particular moments of his own past and project himself into the future.

Question 12

Give an example of priming affecting what someone perceives and an example of priming affecting what someone judges. Why is it significant that neither announces itself?

Show the answerHide the answer

Perception: in repetition priming, a word or object seen recently is identified faster and from briefer or poorer input, so it is literally easier to see the second time. Judgement: in the propaganda effect, a repeated claim comes to feel more likely to be true, even when it is false. (Semantic priming, such as "nurse" speeding recognition of "doctor", or completing a word fragment as "library" after reading about borrowing books, also counts.) It matters that neither announces itself because the influence arrives as a change in how easy something is to process, not as a memory of the earlier exposure. People cannot discount an influence they do not notice, so implicit memory can shape what they see and believe without their knowledge.

Question 13

Name the three types of implicit memory and give an everyday example of each. How do H.M. and Clive Wearing show that implicit memory is separate from explicit memory rather than a weaker form of it?

Show the answerHide the answer

Procedural memory, such as riding a bike or touch-typing; priming, such as completing a word fragment more readily after recently seeing a related word; and classical conditioning, such as feeling uneasy at a smell once associated with something unpleasant. H.M. learned new motor skills across days of practice while having no conscious memory of any practice session, and Clive Wearing still plays the piano and conducts despite dense amnesia. In each case the learning is clearly present in performance while recollection is absent. If implicit memory were only a weaker form of explicit memory, it should fail along with explicit memory; that it survives intact shows it depends on a separate system.

Question 14

Why can an expert often not report what they are doing while doing it? Explain why this follows from what procedural memory is, rather than being a failure of memory.

Show the answerHide the answer

Procedural knowledge is knowing how, and it is often impossible to verbalise even in principle. As a skill becomes well practised it becomes automatic, and automatic performance does not lay down an explicit, episodic record of itself, so there is nothing reportable for the expert to retrieve, sometimes called expert-induced amnesia. The skill is perfectly retained and expressed in performance; it simply is not stored in a form that can be described. The everyday version is a familiar drive that went smoothly but leaves no recollection of the journey.

Question 15

The propaganda effect persists even when people are warned that the information may be false. Explain why, referring to the stage at which the effect operates, and say what this implies for attempts to correct misinformation by restating it.

Show the answerHide the answer

The propaganda effect operates through familiarity rather than belief. Repetition makes a claim easier to process, ease of processing feels like recognition, and recognition feels like truth. The effect arrives as a feeling about the claim at the point of processing, not as a memory of having encountered it or a belief that can be reasoned with, so a warning that engages deliberate scepticism has nothing to act on. For correcting misinformation, this implies that restating a false claim in order to deny it may backfire, because the repetition increases its familiarity. A better strategy is to avoid repeating the false claim and to make the accurate version the familiar one.

Question 16

In what sense does the hippocampus consolidate rather than store memories, and why is that better treated as a slogan than as a precise division of labour?

Show the answerHide the answer

The hippocampus and medial temporal lobe matter above all for forming new declarative memories, consolidating a fragile new experience into a durable trace that comes to be held, over time, in the cortex. This explains why medial temporal damage spares existing skills and much old knowledge while blocking new learning: the warehouse is intact, but the loading dock has gone. It is better treated as a slogan because medial temporal structures also contribute to the storage of declarative memories, so the division between consolidating and storing is one of emphasis, not a clean division of labour.

Question 17

What did Lashley's search for the engram find, and why is a null result about location a positive result about distribution?

Show the answerHide the answer

Lashley trained rats on a complex maze, lesioned different cortical regions in different animals and retested them. He found that the size of the lesion predicted how much memory was lost, and its location did not. Failing to find a place where the memory was stored is informative: if no region is critical and the amount of tissue removed is what matters, the memory must be spread across many regions rather than filed in one. That is the classic demonstration that the engram is distributed, consistent with current evidence that memories are held across networks of cortical and subcortical areas.

Question 18

H.M.'s retrograde amnesia was temporally graded, which is what consolidation theory predicts. Explain why his medication and seizure history nevertheless make this weak evidence for the timescale of consolidation, and relate your answer to the cautions about single cases in §4.3.

Show the answerHide the answer

Consolidation predicts that damage should spare distant memories more than recent ones, and H.M. lost memories from the years before surgery while childhood memories seemed normal. But he had frequent seizures for years before the operation and took a range of anti-epileptic drugs, both of which may have stopped those memories forming properly in the first place. If they were never laid down, their absence says nothing about consolidation, so the gradient may reflect his pre-operative state as much as the loss of his hippocampus. This connects to §4.3: a double dissociation between two patients is strong evidence about architecture because the crossing does the work, but a quantitative claim drawn from inside one patient, such as the timescale of consolidation, is fragile because everything idiosyncratic about that person becomes part of the measurement.

Question 19

What are place cells and grid cells, and what does the Morris water maze show about what the hippocampus contributes to memory? Why is it significant that the deficit is specifically relational?

Show the answerHide the answer

Place cells in the rodent hippocampus fire only when the animal is at a particular location, so the population traces out where the animal is. Grid cells in the entorhinal cortex fire at many locations forming a roughly hexagonal lattice across an environment; the overlap of grids with different spacing and offset indicates position. In the Morris water maze, intact rodents learn to use distant landmarks to swim straight to a hidden platform, whereas animals with hippocampal damage swim aimlessly despite normal vision and swimming. The hippocampus therefore contributes relational binding, linking the elements of an experience to their spatial and temporal context. That the deficit is specifically relational, rather than sensory or motor, matches what place and grid cells compute.

Question 20

Grid cells are often said to supply the coordinate system from which place fields are computed. Explain which part of that claim is well supported and which part the developmental evidence from rat pups calls into question. What role might border cells play?

Show the answerHide the answer

Well supported: grid cells supply the hippocampus with a metric, a scale against which distance and direction can be measured, which is the Mosers' own claim. Called into question: the stronger idea that place fields are simply built by summing several grid patterns. If that were so, grid cells should mature before or with place cells. In rat pups leaving the nest at about two to two and a half weeks, sharp place fields are already present in many hippocampal cells, while grid cells are only weakly periodic and do not become strongly hexagonal until three to four weeks. Border cells, which fire near the geometric boundaries of an environment and are adult-like from a pup's first day outside the nest, may supply some of the input for early place fields. Alternatively, weak spatial input may be sharpened locally within the hippocampus.

Question 21

What are sharp-wave ripples, and what does disrupting them after training show? Explain why this evidence carries extra weight given the weakness of the temporal gradient in H.M.

Show the answerHide the answer

Sharp-wave ripples are brief bursts of high-frequency hippocampal activity during rest and sleep, during which sequences of place cells that fired in earlier exploration are replayed, forwards or in reverse. Their frequency suits the induction of lasting synaptic change in CA1's targets, including distant cortex, which is what a mechanism for gradual cortical recruitment needs. Selectively disrupting ripples during rest after training impairs later memory for the task, establishing a causal link rather than a correlation. This carries extra weight because the classic human evidence, H.M.'s temporal gradient, is compromised by his seizures and medication. The consolidation account therefore rests on a mechanism that can be identified, blocked and shown to be necessary in animals, not only on that fragile case.

Question 22

How do concept cells bear on the choice between population coding, sparse coding and the grandmother cell from Chapter 1?

Show the answerHide the answer

Concept cells in the human temporal lobe fire selectively for one specific person across photographs, the written name and the spoken name, so they code something close to the concept rather than a sensory pattern. They are too selective to be explained by broad population coding. But they are not grandmother cells in the strict sense, because many respond to several related concepts and each concept appears to be carried by many cells rather than by one. That places them at the middle position, sparse coding, which Chapter 1 proposed as the plausible alternative to strict specificity coding.

Question 23

Distinguish habituation from sensitisation, and explain why both are classed as non-associative learning.

Show the answerHide the answer

Habituation is the weakening of a response with repeated presentation of the same stimulus, as when a repeated poke in the back stops making you jump. Sensitisation is the strengthening of a response after a strong or significant stimulus, as when a loud bang makes you startle at a door you would normally ignore. Both are non-associative because neither involves learning a relationship between two things, as classical conditioning does; each is a change in responsiveness to a stimulus on its own.

Question 24

Free recall, cued recall and recognition of the same material give very different scores. What does that tell you about the difference between a memory being available and being accessible?

Show the answerHide the answer

Free recall gives you no support, cued recall gives a hint, and recognition supplies the item itself, and scores rise markedly across the three for the same material. Since nothing is added to memory between the tests, the extra items were stored all along. A memory can be available (present in the store) without being accessible (retrievable under the current conditions). What usually limits performance is access, and the right cue can make an available memory reachable. Apparent forgetting is often a retrieval failure rather than loss.

Question 25

The T-maze result is described as a double dissociation established by lesion rather than by accident. Explain the difference and why it matters for the strength of the inference.

Show the answerHide the answer

In a double dissociation by accident, the evidence comes from patients whose brain damage was caused by injury, disease or surgery, so the location and extent of the damage are not controlled, each case is a sample of one, and something idiosyncratic about a patient may do the work. In the T-maze work the experimenters deliberately lesioned the striatum in some animals and the hippocampus in others: striatal damage impaired habit learning (turning a fixed way for reward) while sparing hippocampus-dependent tasks, and hippocampal damage did the reverse. Because the lesions were chosen and could be applied across groups of animals, the inference that the declarative and procedural systems depend on different structures is much stronger, though the findings still need checking against humans.

Question 26

In what way is the amygdala's contribution to memory different in kind from the hippocampus's and the striatum's?

Show the answerHide the answer

The hippocampus forms new explicit memories, and the striatum supports procedural habits; each is part of the system that forms and holds a particular kind of memory. The amygdala's contribution to emotionally arousing declarative memories is modulatory: it does not appear to store them so much as tune how strongly they are consolidated elsewhere. Amygdala damage in humans removes the normal memory advantage for emotional material rather than abolishing declarative memory. For conditioned fear the amygdala may hold part of the trace itself, since destroying the lateral amygdala neurons recruited during learning can erase an already-learned fear in mice.
