Chapter 5 answers

Long-Term Memory: Encoding, Retrieval and Consolidation

Week 5Chapter 5Tutorial toolsPractice problemsAnswers

Model answers to the 18 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 5. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbook

Question 1

A student cannot recall a term they revised last week. Set out the three stages at which the failure could have occurred, and say what evidence would distinguish them.

Show the answerHide the answer

The failure could be at encoding: the term was never properly learned, for example because it was only read over or rehearsed by rote. It could be at storage: the trace was formed but has degraded or been disrupted, for instance by interference from similar material learned since. Or it could be at retrieval: the trace is intact but inaccessible because the right cue is missing. Evidence that would distinguish them: if a good cue (the first letter, the context in which it was learned, a recognition test) brings the term back, it was stored and the problem was retrieval. If recognition also fails, and the student studied only shallowly, encoding is the likely culprit. If the term was clearly learned (it was recalled on an earlier self-test) and is now lost even to strong cues, a storage problem is more likely. Only a retrieval failure is reversible on the spot.

Question 2

Distinguish maintenance from elaborative rehearsal, and explain why the modal model's treatment of rehearsal was incomplete.

Show the answerHide the answer

Maintenance rehearsal is rote repetition that holds information in mind without engaging its meaning, such as repeating a phone number until you dial it; it keeps the item available briefly but builds little durable trace. Elaborative rehearsal processes meaning and connects the item to existing knowledge, and is far more effective at getting information into long-term memory. The modal model treated rehearsal as a single pump moving information from short-term to long-term memory, implying that more rehearsal means better storage. Because the same time spent in different kinds of rehearsal produces very different results, what matters is the kind of processing, not just its amount.

Question 3

Why does it matter that Craik and Tulving's participants did not know their memory would be tested? What alternative explanation does that design rule out?

Show the answerHide the answer

Because the test was unexpected, participants had no reason to try to learn the words, so the depth of processing was set entirely by the orienting question (structural, phonemic or semantic), not by any intention to memorise. This rules out the explanation that semantic questions produced better memory because people tried harder or used deliberate memorisation strategies on those items. The memory difference can be attributed to the kind of processing itself.

Question 4

The levels-of-processing account has been accused of circularity. Explain the charge precisely, and describe how transfer-appropriate processing meets it. Use Morris and colleagues (1977) in your answer.

Show the answerHide the answer

The charge is that depth is defined by its effect: a process is classed as deep because it produced good memory, and memory is said to be good because the process was deep. Without an independent measure of depth (processing time and effort do not track memory reliably), the theory risks re-describing the result rather than explaining it. Transfer-appropriate processing meets this by making a directional prediction in advance: memory is best when the type of processing at encoding matches that required at test, and this can fail. Morris and colleagues had participants encode words for meaning or for rhyme, then gave either a standard recognition test or a rhyming recognition test. Semantic encoding won on the standard test, but rhyme encoding won on the rhyming test, so the lines crossed. Deep processing was not universally superior; it won when the test demanded meaning.

Question 5

Explain why the self-reference effect produces such good encoding, and connect your answer to what §4.4 said about episodic memory and the self.

Show the answerHide the answer

Judging whether a word describes you produces better recall than ordinary semantic processing. The self is the most richly elaborated knowledge structure anyone has, so relating material to it creates a large number of connections and makes the trace distinctive, which are the two ingredients of deep processing. This connects to §4.4, where episodic memory turned out to be bound up with the self: every episodic memory has you in it at a particular time. Encoding material in relation to yourself attaches it to that same well-organised structure, so the self acts as a powerful encoding device as well as the subject of autobiographical memory.

Question 6

In Karpicke and Roediger (2008), Groups 2 and 3 both reduced their effort. Why did only one of them suffer at the one-week test? What does this tell you about how to revise, and why does it feel wrong?

Show the answerHide the answer

Group 2 stopped restudying pairs once they had been recalled but kept testing all pairs; Group 3 kept restudying all pairs but stopped testing pairs once they had been recalled. A week later Group 2 recalled about 81 per cent, the same as the standard group, while Group 3 fell to about 36 per cent. Repeated retrieval was doing the work, and repeated study of material already learned added almost nothing. So revision should centre on testing yourself repeatedly, including on items you can already recall, rather than rereading. It feels wrong because rereading feels fluent and easy, and fluency is mistaken for learning, whereas retrieval is effortful and exposes what you cannot yet produce, which feels like failing even though it builds durable memory.

Question 7

State the encoding specificity principle and explain, using the divers, why it reframes forgetting as a retrieval problem. Why is the effect weaker for recognition than for free recall?

Show the answerHide the answer

The encoding specificity principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973) states that a retrieval cue is effective to the extent that it overlaps with what was encoded at learning: a cue works by reinstating the original encoding conditions. Godden and Baddeley's divers learned words on land or underwater and recalled better when the test environment matched the learning environment, so the external context had become part of the trace. On this view, much of what we call forgetting is not loss of the trace but a mismatch between the cues available now and those bound in at encoding. The effect is weaker for recognition because the test supplies the item itself, which is already a very strong cue and has less need of context. (A direct replication with sixteen divers did not find the same-context advantage, so the study is best taken as an illustration of the principle.)

Question 8

Why are self-generated retrieval cues more effective than cues generated by someone else, even when the other person's cues are perfectly sensible? Explain what Mäntylä's no-study condition was for and what it rules out, and say what practical advice follows about using a classmate's notes.

Show the answerHide the answer

A cue works by overlapping with the particular way an item was encoded. Cues you generated yourself are guaranteed to match your own encoding, whereas another person's cues, however sensible, reflect their encoding, not yours. Mäntylä found 91 per cent recall of 504 words with self-generated properties as cues, but 55 per cent with another participant's properties. The no-study condition gave the same cues to people who had never seen the words, and they produced the target only 17 per cent of the time. This rules out the explanation that good properties simply describe a word well enough for anyone to reconstruct it: the cues work only in combination with the person's own encoding. Practically, a classmate's notes are someone else's retrieval cues and, on these numbers, worth roughly 60 per cent as much as your own, so it is better to make your own notes and questions, or at least to rework theirs into your own words.

Question 9

Distinguish retroactive from proactive interference, and explain why the two designs are so easily confused. Which memory suffers in each case?

Show the answerHide the answer

In retroactive interference, new learning disrupts retrieval of older material: after learning list B, recall of list A suffers, so the old memory is the casualty (a new phone number blocking the old one). In proactive interference, old learning disrupts retrieval of newer material: having learned list A, recall of list B suffers, so the new memory is the casualty (producing last year's password). The designs are easily confused because both involve learning two similar lists and comparing with a control group that learns only one; the only difference is which list is tested, the earlier one (retroactive) or the later one (proactive).

Question 10

Why is decay so difficult to test as an account of forgetting? Explain how cue overload might unify interference with encoding specificity, and what would be lost if it did.

Show the answerHide the answer

To show decay you would need an interval in which time passed but nothing else happened, with no interfering material. In a waking person time never passes empty, so any forgetting over time can be attributed to interference instead, which is the problem Keppel and Underwood raised for the Brown-Peterson task. Cue overload is the idea that a cue linked to many items points only weakly to each. On this view interference is what happens when the cue-to-item mapping becomes crowded with similar items, so it is a consequence of encoding specificity rather than a separate mechanism. This explains why similarity drives interference and why switching category releases proactive interference. What would be lost is interference as a distinct explanatory mechanism: the two literatures would be describing one phenomenon, and the question of whether interference adds anything beyond cue competition would disappear.

Question 11

Long-term potentiation is said to have the properties a memory mechanism ought to have. Name four such properties and say, for each, why a memory system would require it.

Show the answerHide the answer

It is rapid, which a memory system needs because memories can be formed in a single episode. It is long-lasting, because memories persist. It is input-specific, so strengthening one pathway does not indiscriminately strengthen neighbouring ones, which a memory system needs if different memories are to be kept distinct. And it is associative, so that inputs active together are strengthened together, which is what binding the elements of an experience requires. In the hippocampus, NMDA receptors act as coincidence detectors, opening only when pre- and post-synaptic activity coincide, implementing Hebb's principle.

Question 12

A protein-synthesis inhibitor given after initial learning blocks a new memory but not an old one. In Nader and colleagues (2000) the same drug abolished an old, consolidated memory. What had to be done first, and why does the drug-alone and reminder-alone comparison matter to the argument?

Show the answerHide the answer

Nader and colleagues first had to reactivate the consolidated fear memory by briefly playing the tone the day after conditioning, and only then infuse the protein-synthesis inhibitor into the amygdala. The memory was abolished. The comparison matters because the drug alone does nothing to an old memory (consistent with protein-synthesis inhibitors blocking only new consolidation) and the reminder alone does nothing lasting. Only the combination destroyed the memory. The most economical explanation is that reactivation returned the memory to a labile, protein-synthesis-dependent state, so it had to be re-stored, and blocking synthesis during that window prevented re-storage. Retrieval reopened the memory rather than simply reading it out.

Question 13

Contrast the standard and multiple-trace models of systems consolidation. What pattern of retrograde amnesia would count as evidence for each, and why does the doubt over H.M.'s temporal gradient matter here?

Show the answerHide the answer

On the standard model the hippocampus rapidly binds the distributed cortical fragments of an experience, and through repeated reactivation cortico-cortical connections strengthen until the cortex supports the memory alone, so hippocampal dependence is temporary. On the multiple-trace model the hippocampus never fully relinquishes detailed episodic memories: each retrieval lays down a new trace, so vivid episodic memories stay hippocampus-dependent however old they are, and only semantic gist becomes independent. The standard model predicts a temporal gradient, with remote memories spared after hippocampal damage. The multiple-trace model predicts that episodic detail is lost across the whole lifespan while semantic gist shows a gradient. The doubt over H.M. matters because his gradient is the standard model's headline human evidence, and years of seizures and medication before surgery may have prevented recent memories forming at all, so the case carries less weight than usually assumed.

Question 14

Sleep is said to support consolidation both passively and actively. Explain both routes, and say why targeted memory reactivation is stronger evidence for the active one than replay observations alone.

Show the answerHide the answer

Passively, sleep protects a fragile trace by removing the interference that waking experience would otherwise impose. Actively, during slow-wave sleep the hippocampus replays recent learning in sharp-wave ripples, in effect training the cortex. Observing replay alone shows only a correlation between replay and later memory. In targeted memory reactivation, a cue (an odour or a sound) is paired with material at learning and re-presented during sleep; cued material is remembered better, and Rudoy and colleagues found the benefit for the specific cued object locations. Because the experimenter controls which memories are reactivated and the benefit follows that choice, this turns the active account from an inference into a manipulation. A meta-analysis of about ninety studies finds a small-to-moderate benefit, clearest when cues are given in non-REM sleep.

Question 15

The crossover in Morris and colleagues (1977) appeared only for congruent acquisition items. Explain what congruency means in that design, and discuss how far this qualification weakens the case for transfer-appropriate processing.

Show the answerHide the answer

Each word arrived in a frame it either fitted or did not: a sentence it completed sensibly or did not, or a word it rhymed with or did not. Congruent items are those where the answer was yes, so the word was integrated with its frame at encoding; incongruent items were merely judged against it. The crossover, rhyme encoding beating semantic encoding on the rhyming test, appeared only for congruent items. For incongruent items semantic encoding won on both tests. This narrows the case rather than refuting it: the most cited demonstration holds only when the item was integrated with its frame, and Morris and colleagues said the role of congruency needed clarification. The principle survives, but the famous crossover is conditional, and it is unwise to rest a strong argument on it alone.

Question 16

Explain how reconsolidation could account for the finding that flashbulb memories drift while remaining vividly confident, and say what evidence would be needed to show that it does.

Show the answerHide the answer

Flashbulb memories feel photographic and are held with high confidence, yet they drift and distort over time. On a reconsolidation account, every time you retell or recall the event you reactivate the memory and return it to a labile state, and what is re-stored is the version you have just told, including any changes. Repeated retellings would therefore accumulate drift, while the vividness and rehearsal keep confidence high. To show that this actually happens, you would need evidence that recalling the memory opens it to modification in humans: for example, that the amount of drift tracks the number of retellings, that new information introduced around a retelling is absorbed into the memory (as in Hupbach and colleagues' reminder effect), or that blocking re-storage after reactivation alters the memory. The account is plausible but has not been directly tested for flashbulb memories.

Question 17

Set out the four routes by which a reactivated memory might be modified, and name one practical and one ethical obstacle to using any of them clinically.

Show the answerHide the answer

Sandrini and colleagues (2018) describe four routes. Behavioural means, such as interference or extinction, supply new content during the reconsolidation window, close to what exposure therapy does. Stressor events alter the hormonal conditions under which re-storage happens. Pharmacological agents act on the biology of re-storage, for example propranolol given after reactivation to blunt the noradrenergic arousal that would strengthen the memory. Non-invasive brain stimulation (tDCS or TMS) targets the regions involved in reactivation. A practical obstacle: reactivation does not reliably open a reconsolidation window, since it depends on timing, how strongly the memory is reactivated and how old and strong it is, and brain-stimulation effects vary considerably between individuals. Reactivating a traumatic memory is also itself distressing. An ethical obstacle: a memory is a record and sometimes evidence as well as a burden, so deciding when a memory should be weakened, and who decides, is not something the science can settle.

Question 18

Design a two-week revision plan for this unit that uses at least five principles from this chapter. Justify each choice by naming the principle it exploits, and say which of your choices will feel least productive while working best.

Show the answerHide the answer

One model plan. Days 1 to 3: for each chapter, write your own questions and summary cards in your own words, with properties or cues you generate yourself (generation effect; self-generated cues, Mäntylä). Organise each chapter's material under its main headings and link examples to your own experience (organisation; self-reference; elaboration). From day 2 onward, start each session by answering yesterday's questions from memory before looking at notes, and keep testing items even after you get them right (testing effect; Karpicke and Roediger). Spread each chapter across several sessions separated by days instead of finishing one chapter per day, interleaving chapters within a session (spacing effect). Write practice answers in the format you will actually be asked to produce, timed and closed-book (transfer-appropriate processing; encoding specificity, by studying in conditions like the test). Make vivid interactive images for lists of terms or studies (imagery). Sleep normally throughout and avoid an all-nighter before the test (sleep-dependent consolidation). The choices that feel least productive while working best are retrieval practice on items you already know and spacing, because both feel effortful and slow compared with fluent rereading, yet they produce the most durable learning.
