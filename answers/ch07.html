Conceptual Knowledge

Questions in the textbook

Question 1

Explain what is meant by calling a concept a pointer to knowledge, using an example of an object you have never encountered.

Show the answerHide the answer

A concept is a mental representation of a category, and knowing that something belongs to it gives access to a wealth of general knowledge that did not come from the object itself. If you are told that an unfamiliar vehicle is a truck, you immediately know it has wheels and an engine, carries loads, runs on fuel and behaves in particular ways in traffic, even though you have never seen that truck. The concept points to stored knowledge that can be applied to the new instance, which is what lets us generalise from a few instances to an effectively unlimited number of new ones, while still noticing what is distinctive about a particular one.

Question 2

Why does the definitional approach succeed for square but fail for bird? What does family resemblance offer instead, and what does it cost us in precision?

Show the answerHide the answer

The definitional approach requires features that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient. Square has such a definition (a four-sided polygon with equal sides and right angles), so anything meeting it is a square and nothing else is. For bird, any plausible definition fails: if birds fly, the penguin is excluded although it is clearly a bird. Necessary features can often be found, but a set that is also sufficient usually cannot. Family resemblance (Wittgenstein; Rosch and Mervis) holds that members share overlapping features without any one feature being common to all, so membership is graded rather than all-or-none. The cost is precision: there is no sharp boundary or test that settles membership, so borderline cases are genuinely uncertain.

Question 3

Rosch and Mervis gave a two-part account of typicality. State both parts, and use them to explain why a penguin is a poor bird in a way that mentioning flight alone does not capture.

Show the answerHide the answer

An item is typical to the extent that (1) it has features that are frequent in its own category and (2) it lacks features that are frequent in other categories. The robin has many common bird features and few features belonging elsewhere, so it is highly typical. The penguin is a poor bird not only because it lacks a common bird feature, flight, but because it has features frequent in other categories: it swims and has fin-like wings, which belong more to fish and marine animals. Mentioning flight captures only the first half of the account; typicality depends on contrast with other categories as well as resemblance within one.

Question 4

Describe Rosch's priming study and explain precisely what it rules out. Why could a prototype that was merely a stored description not produce this result?

Show the answerHide the answer

Participants heard a colour name such as "green" and then judged whether two colour patches were the same or different. On same trials they were faster when the colours were good, prototypical greens than when they were poor off-greens. This rules out the view that a prototype is only a description that can be produced on request: if it were, hearing the word would not change how quickly two colour patches could be compared. The category name must have activated a representation that was already biasing perception before the colours appeared, so the prototype behaves as a live, active representation rather than a stored summary waiting to be consulted.

Question 5

A robin is judged a better bird than a penguin. Explain this on the prototype account and on the exemplar account. What can the exemplar account do that the prototype account finds awkward, and why?

Show the answerHide the answer

On the prototype account, the robin matches the weighted-feature summary of birds closely, while the penguin matches it poorly, so the robin is more typical and verified faster. On the exemplar account, the robin resembles many of the specific birds you have stored, while the penguin resembles few of them. The exemplar account can handle atypical members gracefully, because a penguin can be stored and recognised as itself rather than only as a poor match to an average, and it copes with highly variable categories whose members cannot be sensibly averaged. The prototype account finds these awkward because a single summary discards information about the exceptions and the diversity of the category.

Question 6

Why is the prototype approach better suited to large coherent categories and the exemplar approach to small varied ones? Use furniture in your answer.

Show the answerHide the answer

In a large, coherent category, members share many features, so an average is informative and storing one summary is efficient: the prototype captures most of what matters. In a small or varied category the members are too diverse to average sensibly, and remembering specific instances preserves information that an average would lose. Furniture is a varied category: what would the average of a wardrobe and a beanbag look like? A single prototype of furniture would be uninformative, whereas stored exemplars (chairs, tables, sofas, wardrobes) let new items be compared with the members they most resemble. People seem to use both strategies, leaning on abstraction when categories are tight and on instances when they are not.

Question 7

Asked what an object is, people say chair rather than furniture or kitchen chair. Explain the information trade-off that makes the basic level special, and why the trade-off is asymmetric.

Show the answerHide the answer

The basic level (chair) is the level of maximum information for minimum cost. Moving up to the superordinate level (furniture) loses a great deal of information, because members of furniture share far fewer features than members of chair. Moving down to the subordinate level (kitchen chair) adds very little, because a kitchen chair shares almost all its features with chairs in general. The asymmetry, a large loss upward and a small gain downward, makes the basic level the most efficient level at which to categorise, which is why people name objects there spontaneously, children learn it first, and it is the level at which a category can be used in a uniform way.

Question 8

What happens to the basic level with expertise, and why does that finding tell us the privileged level is a fact about the knower rather than about the world?

Show the answerHide the answer

With expertise the basic level moves down: a birdwatcher names a song thrush as readily and quickly as a novice says "bird" (Tanaka & Taylor, 1991). If the privileged level were a property of the world, of how features are actually distributed across objects, expertise could not move it, because the objects have not changed. That it moves shows the basic level reflects how finely a particular person has learned to carve up a domain, a fact about the knower. Training experts is therefore partly a matter of training new basic-level categories.

Question 9

Collins and Quillian predicted that “a canary has skin” takes longer to verify than “a canary can sing”. Explain the prediction and the principle of cognitive economy behind it.

Show the answerHide the answer

In Collins and Quillian's network, properties are stored at the highest node to which they apply (cognitive economy): "can sing" is stored at CANARY, "can fly" at BIRD and "has skin" at ANIMAL, rather than repeating every property at every node. To verify a statement you traverse links from CANARY to the node holding the property. "A canary can sing" requires no traversal, while "a canary has skin" requires crossing two links, so the model predicts longer verification times the further the property is stored, and sentence-verification times showed this distance effect.

Question 10

Give two findings the strict hierarchy could not handle, and explain how spreading activation addresses each.

Show the answerHide the answer

First, typicality: CANARY and OSTRICH are each one link below BIRD, yet "a canary is a bird" is verified faster than "an ostrich is a bird". Second, reversed distance: "a pig is an animal" is verified faster than "a pig is a mammal", although MAMMAL is closer to PIG in the hierarchy (Rips et al., 1973). (In addition, Conrad showed that the distance effect itself was confounded with association strength.) Spreading activation replaces the hierarchy with a network organised by semantic relatedness, in which more closely associated concepts are joined by shorter or stronger links. Canary is more closely linked to bird than ostrich is, so activation reaches BIRD faster. And pig is judged closer in meaning to animal than to mammal, so the link is shorter. Both results become differences in link strength and distance rather than levels in a tree.

Question 11

What is graceful degradation, and why does it make connectionist models attractive as accounts of the brain? Why can a node-based model not produce it as easily?

Show the answerHide the answer

Graceful degradation means that damage to part of a system degrades its performance gradually and broadly rather than deleting particular items outright. In a connectionist model knowledge is distributed across the weights of many connections, so no fact lives in one place, and removing some units or connections makes everything slightly worse rather than making specific facts vanish. That resembles how real brain damage usually behaves. In a node-based model each concept or fact sits at a particular node, so damaging that node removes that item specifically while leaving the rest intact, which does not naturally produce a gradual, general decline.

Question 12

Connectionist networks usually need many exposures to learn, whereas people sometimes learn a concept in one trial. Explain why this weakness has the same source as the model's strengths.

Show the answerHide the answer

Connectionist networks learn by gradually adjusting the weights of many connections in response to repeated experience. Because knowledge is spread across shared weights that are changed little by little, the network generalises (learning about one concept informs related ones that share weights) and degrades gracefully (no single weight is critical). The same gradual, distributed weight change means that a single exposure moves the weights only slightly, so many exposures are needed to learn. The strengths and the weakness come from the same property.

Question 13

Set out the similarities and the three main differences between semantic-network and connectionist models, and say which handles typicality better and why.

Show the answerHide the answer

Similarities: both represent knowledge in interconnected units and both stress the importance of relations between concepts. Differences: (1) structure: semantic networks are organised and often hierarchical, while connectionist networks are distributed and parallel, arranged in layers; (2) where knowledge lives: in labelled nodes and links versus in the weights of connections across many units; (3) learning: semantic networks were specified by the modeller, while connectionist networks acquire their weights gradually from experience. Connectionist models handle typicality better, because typicality emerges naturally as graded patterns of activation, whereas the strict semantic hierarchy could not explain it at all; spreading activation addressed it only by adding graded link strengths.

Question 14

Patients K.C. and E.W. could name cars, tables, tomatoes and pears but not animals. Explain what this pattern shows and why a double dissociation is needed rather than a single case.

Show the answerHide the answer

The pattern shows a category-specific deficit: knowledge of animals was impaired while knowledge of non-living things and of fruit and vegetables was spared, so conceptual knowledge is not held in one undifferentiated store and different kinds of knowledge depend on partly different tissue. A single case is not enough, because animals might simply be harder to name, for example more visually similar to one another, so any damage would hit them first. The reverse pattern in other patients (Warrington & McCarthy), impaired naming of man-made objects with living things spared, rules out an explanation in terms of difficulty and completes a double dissociation. (This K.C. is not the K.C. of Chapter 4.)

Question 15

Distinguish the sensory-functional, multiple-factor and semantic-category accounts of category-specific deficits. What does it suggest that each explains part of the data and none all of it?

Show the answerHide the answer

The sensory-functional hypothesis holds that living things are known mainly by their sensory properties and artifacts mainly by their function, so damage to sensory stores impairs living things and damage to functional stores impairs artifacts. The multiple-factor approach says concepts are distinguished along several dimensions at once, with animals weighted towards motion, colour and size and artifacts towards actions performed with them. The semantic-category approach proposes dedicated neural circuits for evolutionarily important categories such as animals and plant foods. The sensory-functional account struggles with K.C. and E.W., whose fruit and vegetable naming, things known largely by appearance and taste, was spared. That each account explains part of the data and none all of it suggests the organising principle may be more general than any of them, consistent with a model in which knowledge depends on several modality-specific regions bound together.

Question 16

Give one piece of evidence for the embodied approach, and state the strongest objection to it. How does weak embodiment respond?

Show the answerHide the answer

Evidence: when people read action words such as lick, pick and kick, they differentially engage the face, hand and leg regions of motor cortex in the same somatotopic order as the actions themselves (semantic somatotopy; Hauk et al., 2004). Mirror neurons, which fire both when an action is performed and when it is observed, offer a possible mechanism. The strongest objection concerns abstract concepts, such as justice or prime number, which are hard to ground in sensorimotor experience; in addition, mirror neurons may accompany understanding rather than contribute to it. Weak embodiment accepts that concepts are grounded in perception and action but allows that some knowledge is genuinely abstracted away from the body, which is the position the hub-and-spoke model takes.

Question 17

rTMS over the anterior temporal lobe slows naming for all categories; over the parietal lobe it slows only tools. Explain how this pattern identifies the hub and the spoke, why the identification does not have to be assumed in advance, and what control shows that the anterior temporal effect is semantic rather than a general slowing of speech.

Show the answerHide the answer

Pobric and colleagues found that rTMS over the left anterior temporal lobe slowed naming of both living and man-made things, a category-general effect, while rTMS over the left inferior parietal lobe slowed naming only of manipulable man-made objects such as tools. If all concepts route through a hub, disrupting it should impair every category; disrupting a spoke should impair only the concepts that depend on it, here knowledge of how objects are manipulated. The hub is identified by which disruption is category-general and specifically semantic, so it is not assumed in advance. Category-general slowing could also arise from disrupting a general stage of naming, such as retrieving or articulating words. The control task showed anterior temporal stimulation slowed naming pictures, which requires retrieving a concept, but not naming numbers, which requires the same spoken output with little conceptual retrieval, so the effect is semantic.

Question 18

Why is the hub-and-spoke model described as a middle position rather than a rival to the embodied approach?

Show the answerHide the answer

The hub-and-spoke model accepts the central embodied insight: the spokes are modality-specific regions for vision, motion, sound, action, words, emotion and taste, so concepts remain grounded in perception and action. It adds a transmodal hub in the anterior temporal lobes that binds the spokes into coherent concepts that can transfer across modalities and generalise across members, which is what abstraction requires and strong embodiment struggles to deliver. It therefore absorbs the grounding claim rather than rejecting it, committing to weak embodiment, and its evidence counts against strong embodiment as much as against fully disembodied symbols.

Question 19

Using typicality and the basic level, explain both why an experienced clinician is fast and accurate and why they are most at risk of missing an atypical case. Why is this not a failure of diligence?

Show the answerHide the answer

Expertise moves the basic level downward, so the experienced clinician recognises a specific condition as quickly and fluently as a novice recognises a coarse category, matching the case to a rich stored prototype or set of exemplars. Because that matching runs on typicality, typical presentations are recognised quickly and correctly. The same mechanism means atypical presentations, such as a heart attack without chest pain, match the prototype poorly and are more likely to be missed or misfiled, and the expert's fast, fluent judgement makes this harder to notice. This is not a failure of diligence: it is the cost of the mechanism that makes expert judgement fast and usually right. A good clinician learns to distrust the fluent prototypical match when the stakes are high.

Question 20

Explain how a prototype becomes a stereotype, and say what is and is not different about the cognitive processes involved.

Show the answerHide the answer

When the category is a kind of person, the prototype is a weighted summary of features thought typical of that group, and applying it to individuals is a stereotype: it speeds judgement and favours the typical member while resisting atypical or borderline ones, just as with birds. The cognitive processes are the ordinary ones of categorisation (graded similarity, typicality and fast automatic matching), so nothing special is needed to produce a stereotype. What is different is the object of categorisation: the people being categorised have an interest in the outcome that chairs and birds do not, so the same mechanism has social and ethical consequences.
