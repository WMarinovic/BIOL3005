Chapter 10 answers

Problem Solving and Creativity

Week 10Chapter 10Tutorial toolsPractice problemsAnswers

Questions in the textbook

Question 1

Distinguish well-defined from ill-defined problems, and explain why the information-processing approach suits the former better. Why does the nesting of well-defined sub-problems inside ill-defined ones matter for the debate between the two accounts?

Show the answerHide the answer

Well-defined problems have a clearly specified starting state, a finite set of legal moves (operators) and an unambiguous goal, such as an algebra equation or the Tower of Hanoi. Ill-defined problems lack one or more of these, such as "paint a beautiful picture", which has no agreed goal and no fixed operators. The information-processing approach models solving as searching a problem space from start to goal using operators, so it needs those elements to be specified, which is true of well-defined problems. The nesting matters because most real and creative problems are ill-defined but contain well-defined sub-problems. The search account describes the nested sub-problems well, and the Gestalt restructuring account describes the work of deciding what the sub-problems are, so the two accounts divide the territory rather than compete for it.

Question 2

Explain the difference between an algorithm and a heuristic, and what each buys and costs.

Show the answerHide the answer

An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure guaranteed to produce the correct result for a well-defined problem, like a recipe. It buys certainty, but it must be followed exactly and for large problem spaces it can be impossibly slow. A heuristic is a rule of thumb that often works but is not guaranteed, such as working backwards or breaking a task into subgoals. It buys speed and economy, and is used when there is too much information, too little time, too little information or little at stake, but it costs reliability: its occasional failures are the source of systematic biases.

Question 3

What is means–ends analysis, and how do subgoals help solve the Tower of Hanoi? Why does exhaustive search fail for the travelling salesperson problem?

Show the answerHide the answer

Means-ends analysis is a heuristic in which, at each step, you identify the largest difference between your current state and the goal and choose an operator that reduces it; if no single move can reduce it, you set a subgoal, a nearer state that makes the reducing move possible. In the Tower of Hanoi you cannot move the largest disc until the smaller ones are elsewhere, so you set the subgoal of moving the smaller discs to the middle peg, move the largest disc to the third peg, then rebuild on top of it; applied recursively this solves the three-disc puzzle in seven moves. Exhaustive search fails for the travelling salesperson problem because the number of possible routes explodes so fast with the number of cities that checking them all is infeasible, even though the rules are clear. People nonetheless find near-optimal routes, apparently by perceiving global spatial structure.

Question 4

Describe Metcalfe and Wiebe's (1987) warmth-rating result. Why is asking people repeatedly how close they are better evidence than asking them whether insight felt sudden?

Show the answerHide the answer

Participants worked on insight problems and non-insight algebra problems and rated every fifteen seconds how warm, or close to a solution, they felt. For algebra problems warmth rose steadily as they approached the answer; for insight problems it stayed low and flat until it jumped in the final interval before the solution. Asking whether insight felt sudden would yield a report about a feeling, which could reflect expectations or how the experience is remembered. Repeated warmth ratings can be compared with actual progress, and showed that people's metacognition tracked progress for non-insight problems but not for insight problems. That is a behavioural difference between problem types, not merely a reported one.

Question 5

State the deflationary interpretation of insight, and say what the flat warmth curve would mean on that reading.

Show the answerHide the answer

The deflationary interpretation is that all problems are solved by unconscious incremental processing, and the insight "Aha!" is simply the moment that processing crosses into awareness, not a distinct mechanism. On that reading the flat warmth curve does not mean no progress was being made; it means the solver had no conscious access to the progress being made unconsciously. In the book's terms, the incremental work is the Elephant's and the Rider meets the answer only when it arrives.

Question 6

Köhler read Sultan's behaviour as insight. How do the circumstances of Sultan's two-stick solution, and Birch's (1945) finding on prior experience, complicate that interpretation?

Show the answerHide the answer

Köhler read Sultan's abrupt, purposeful stacking of boxes or joining of two sticks as insight rather than trial and error. But by Köhler's own account the two-stick solution came after an hour of failed attempts, while Sultan was idly handling the sticks, so it may have been reached through manipulation rather than sudden restructuring. Birch found that chimpanzees with no prior experience of playing with sticks rarely solved stick problems at all. On this evidence insight may build on earlier trial-and-error experience rather than replace it.

Question 7

Explain, using the mutilated-checkerboard problem, why a change of representation can turn an intractable problem into an obvious one. What information does the useful representation discard, and why is discarding it helpful?

Show the answerHide the answer

Remove two diagonally opposite corners from a 64-square board, leaving 62, and ask whether 31 dominoes, each covering two adjacent squares, can tile them. Represented by position, the problem invites endless attempts at arrangements. Represented by colour, it is obvious: every domino covers one light and one dark square, but the two removed corners are the same colour, so the board has 32 squares of one colour and 30 of the other, and no tiling can succeed. The useful representation discards almost all the detail people fixate on, the whole geometry of the board, and keeps one fact, the colour count. Discarding detail helps because the irrelevant information was what made the search seem necessary and endless; a good representation is more selective rather than richer.

Question 8

What is functional fixedness, and how do the candle and two-string problems demonstrate it?

Show the answerHide the answer

Functional fixedness is the difficulty of using an object for anything other than its usual function, a special case of mental set. In Duncker's candle problem, people must fix a candle to a wall with a box of tacks and matches; they struggle because they see the box as a container for tacks rather than as a platform that can be tacked to the wall. In Maier's two-string problem, two strings hang too far apart to grasp both; the solution is to tie the pliers to one string and swing it as a pendulum, which is hard because the pliers are seen as a tool rather than a weight.

Question 9

Using Table 10.1, explain how problems 2 to 6 create the mental set, why problems 7 and 8 fail to break it even though a two-pour route exists, and why problem 9 exposes it. What does the untrained control group contribute to the conclusion?

Show the answerHide the answer

Problems 2 to 6 are all solved by B minus A minus 2C, and for most of them there is no shorter route, so five consecutive successes make that routine simply what a water-jug problem is. In problems 7 and 8 a two-pour route exists (A minus C, A plus C), but the long method also gives the right answer, so nothing signals that anything simpler is available, and being right is mistaken for being efficient. In problem 9 the long method fails (it gives 42, not 25), and only A minus C works, so the set becomes visible as participants fail, take far longer, or retry the long method. The untrained control group, who did not see problems 2 to 6, tend to find the simple routes straight away, which shows the difficulty was created by the training sequence rather than by the problems themselves: it is negative transfer from earlier success.

Question 10

Problem 5 has a shorter route that participants almost never find, while problem 7 has a much shorter one they also miss. What does the difference between those two cases suggest about what has to be true of an alternative before a set will be broken?

Show the answerHide the answer

In problem 5 the shorter route (A plus 2C) saves only one pour and appears while the routine is still building, so it is not conspicuous enough to interrupt a method that is working. In problem 7 the alternative saves two pours, yet is still missed because the long method also works. Neither inefficiency nor the availability of a simpler route is enough to break a set while the established method still succeeds. What seems to be required is that the established method actually fails, as in problem 9, or that the alternative is made salient. A set is broken by failure, not by the mere existence of something better.

Question 11

Set out the three steps of analogical transfer. Given that a third solved the radiation problem after reading the fortress story but three-quarters solved it when told the story was relevant, what kind of failure is the main obstacle?

Show the answerHide the answer

Analogical transfer proceeds by noticing that a source problem is relevant, mapping its parts onto the target (fortress to tumour, army to ray, mined roads to healthy tissue), and applying the mapping to generate a parallel solution (several weak beams converging on the tumour). About a tenth solved the radiation problem cold, a third after reading the fortress story, and three-quarters when told the story was relevant. Once told, most people could map and apply the analogy, so the main obstacle is noticing: a retrieval failure, not a failure to understand the story or the analogy.

Question 12

How does comparing two source problems improve transfer, and what is a problem schema? State the analogical paradox and give two explanations for it.

Show the answerHide the answer

Comparing two analogous source stories forces out their shared deep structure and strips away the surface details on which they differ, roughly doubling transfer (Gick & Holyoak; Gentner and colleagues' analogical encoding). What is extracted is a problem schema, an abstract representation of the structure common to a class of problems, which can then be recognised in a new problem. The analogical paradox (Dunbar, 2001) is that analogical transfer is fragile in the laboratory, where people cling to surface similarity, yet scientists, politicians and engineers use analogies fluently in the real world. Explanations: expertise means the deep structure of problems in one's own domain is what is noticed first; and laboratory problems are stripped of the rich context that, in real settings, cues retrieval of the right source. (Dunbar also found that working scientists mostly draw analogies from closely related systems.)

Question 13

How do experts differ from novices in the chess-reconstruction studies, and what exactly does the random-board control rule out?

Show the answerHide the answer

When shown a board for a few seconds, experts reconstructed real game positions far better than novices, but on randomly arranged pieces they were no better, or only slightly better in larger samples. Experts store tens of thousands of chunks, recurring meaningful configurations of pieces, so a real position can be encoded in a few chunks. The random-board control largely rules out superior general visual memory as the explanation, because better memory would help on any board. What remains is the recognition of meaningful patterns, which explains why expertise is context-bound.

Question 14

What is the difference between sorting problems by surface and by deep structure, and how does it connect expertise to analogical noticing?

Show the answerHide the answer

Surface structure refers to the objects and features a problem mentions, such as inclined planes or springs; deep structure refers to the underlying principle it turns on, such as conservation of energy. Chi and colleagues found novices sorted physics problems by surface features and experts by deep principles. Because experts represent new problems by their deep structure, they connect them to relevant knowledge and to analogous problems already solved, which is precisely the noticing step that novices find hard in analogical transfer. Expert problem representation and expert analogical noticing are the same ability, which also helps explain the analogical paradox.

Question 15

Where would you expect an expert's mental set to be most costly? Describe a situation in which a novice might outperform an expert.

Show the answerHide the answer

An expert's mental set is most costly when a problem presents familiar surface features but requires an unfamiliar principle or approach: the expert's rich, automatic domain knowledge immediately suggests the standard approach, which locks them into a familiar but wrong method, as in Luchins' studies. For example, a clinician might quickly diagnose a common condition from a typical-looking presentation when the case is actually something rarer that requires a different line of investigation, or an experienced engineer might apply a standard design to a problem whose constraints have changed. A novice, lacking the entrenched routine, may approach the problem fresh and notice the simpler or correct solution, much as the untrained control participants found the two-pour routes.

Question 16

Define divergent and convergent thinking, and name the four dimensions on which Unusual Uses responses are scored.

Show the answerHide the answer

Divergent thinking is open-ended thinking that generates many possible answers, none simply right or wrong; convergent thinking narrows towards a single correct answer. Unusual (Alternative) Uses responses are scored on fluency (how many responses), originality (how rare they are), flexibility (how many distinct categories they span) and elaboration (how much detail they contain).

Question 17

Why should you be cautious about treating divergent-thinking scores as a measure of creativity? What is the gap between the task and the construct?

Show the answerHide the answer

Divergent-thinking tasks measure the ability to produce many varied responses to a short prompt under time pressure in a laboratory. Creativity, defined as producing ideas that are original, useful and surprising, typically involves sustained, knowledge-heavy work in a real domain, evaluated by others, and requires evaluation and refinement (convergent processes) as well as generation. The correlations between divergent-thinking scores and real-world creative achievement are modest. So a divergent-thinking score is a claim about a laboratory task, not about creative work, and claims that a person or group is "more creative" should be checked against what was actually measured.

Question 18

Summarise the evidence that the default mode and executive control networks both contribute to creative cognition, and explain why their cooperation is surprising.

Show the answerHide the answer

Ellamil and colleagues had art students alternate between generating and evaluating ideas for book covers in the scanner: generation recruited medial temporal memory regions, while evaluation recruited executive and default-mode regions together, and the two networks were positively coupled throughout. Mayseless and colleagues found that more original Alternative Uses responses were associated with greater activity in default-mode regions such as medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortex. Beaty and colleagues found the default mode network coupling with salience regions and later with executive regions during divergent thinking. Their cooperation is surprising because the default mode network (active during rest and self-generated thought) and the executive control network (active during focused goal-directed control) are normally anti-correlated: states that engage one usually suppress the other.

Question 19

Why is the pre-stimulus finding in the prepared-mind studies more surprising than the gamma burst at the moment of insight?

Show the answerHide the answer

The gamma burst over right anterior temporal cortex about a third of a second before an insight solution is reported is a correlate of the solution itself, so it is not surprising that solving by insight has a neural signature at that moment. The pre-stimulus finding is that brain activity in the seconds before the problem even appears predicts how it will be solved: more frontal and midline activity precedes insight solutions, more occipital activity precedes analytic ones. The state of the brain going into a problem biases the route to its solution before there is any problem to solve, which echoes the anticipatory preparation of Chapter 2.

Question 20

The default mode and executive control networks are normally anti-correlated, yet both contribute to creative cognition. Explain why that raises a question about mechanism, and what role the salience network is proposed to play. What direct evidence supports the proposal, and why is it still not settled?

Show the answerHide the answer

If the default mode and executive control networks normally suppress each other, there must be some mechanism that lets them work together during creative cognition rather than competing. The triple-network model proposes that a third network, the salience network and particularly the anterior insula, acts as a switch, engaging the executive network and standing down the default mode network when something salient arrives, so flexible creative thought might depend on how well switching between them is controlled. Direct support: during divergent thinking the default mode network couples first with salience regions, including the insula, and later with executive regions (Beaty et al., 2015). It is not settled because the triple-network model was developed to explain clinical conditions rather than creativity, and the evidence that switching causes creative cognition is limited.

Question 21

Give the four standard explanations for why group brainstorming underperforms individuals working alone, and say which remedy addresses which mechanism.

Show the answerHide the answer

Production blocking: only one person can speak at a time, so others must hold ideas in limited working memory while waiting and often lose them; generating alone first and pooling afterwards removes it. Evaluation apprehension: people withhold ideas that might look foolish, which selectively removes the most original contributions; collecting ideas anonymously or in writing reduces it. Social loafing: contributions are not individually identifiable, so effort falls; making each person's contributions identifiable to the facilitator, even if anonymous to the group, reduces it. Conformity and anchoring: early ideas set a direction that later ideas cluster around, convergence arriving too early; writing ideas independently before sharing them removes anchoring on whoever speaks first. More generally, separate the generation phase from the evaluation phase.

Question 22

Barack and colleagues (2024) found that higher attention-deficit traits predicted more exploration and higher reward rates in a foraging task. Explain precisely why this does not establish that the traits were evolutionarily selected for, and what evidence would be needed.

Show the answerHide the answer

The finding is a correlation between traits, measured by screening rather than diagnosis, and performance in a modern computerised task. It shows there are environments in which these traits pay off. Selection for a trait is a claim about ancestral environments and differential reproductive success over many generations, which a modern laboratory task cannot reveal, and ancestral selection pressures leave no direct record. "This trait helps in this task" and "this trait was selected for" are different claims. Supporting the evolutionary claim would require evidence about the genetic basis of the traits, their fitness consequences in ancestral-like environments, and signatures of selection on the relevant genes, which is extremely hard to obtain.

Question 23

A claim that a group is “more creative” rests on divergent-thinking scores. Set out two distinct reasons for caution, one about the sample and one about the measure.

Show the answerHide the answer

The sample: many studies use subclinical groups, people with elevated traits rather than a clinical diagnosis, or self-selected samples, so results may not generalise to the group the claim refers to, and the advantage may be specific to particular domains or motivational conditions. For ADHD, Hoogman and colleagues found divergent thinking somewhat elevated in subclinical groups, convergent thinking not enhanced. The measure: divergent-thinking scores measure producing many varied responses to a short prompt under time pressure, which predicts real-world creative achievement only modestly. So a higher score supports a claim about a laboratory task, not about creative work in a real domain.
