Chapter 11 answers

Judgement, Decisions and Reasoning

Week 11Chapter 11Tutorial toolsPractice problemsAnswers

Questions in the textbook

Question 1

Distinguish inductive from deductive reasoning, and name the three factors that determine the strength of an inductive argument.

Show the answerHide the answer

Deductive reasoning starts from general premises and derives a conclusion that is guaranteed to be true if the premises are true. Inductive reasoning starts from specific observations and generalises to a conclusion that is probable but never guaranteed: a single black swan overturns "all swans are white". The defining difference is certainty rather than direction. The strength of an inductive argument depends on the representativeness of the observations (are they typical of the whole, or a skewed sample?), the number of observations, and the quality of the evidence (precise, well controlled and from good sources rather than casual impressions).

Question 2

Explain the difference between validity and truth, using the “all flowers are animals” syllogism. Why is belief bias evidence that we judge form by content?

Show the answerHide the answer

Validity concerns form: a syllogism is valid if the conclusion follows from the premises, so that if the premises were true the conclusion would have to be true. Truth concerns content: whether the premises actually hold. "All flowers are animals; all animals can jump; therefore all flowers can jump" has false premises and an absurd conclusion, yet is valid, because the conclusion follows from the premises. Belief bias is the tendency to judge a syllogism valid when its conclusion is believable and invalid when it is not, regardless of the logic: many people reject the valid "some bananas are pink" conclusion because it is unbelievable. Since validity depends only on form, judging it by believability shows people are evaluating content when they are supposed to evaluate form.

Question 3

Using Figure 11.1, explain why “all mammals are animals; all dogs are animals; therefore all dogs are mammals” is invalid even though its conclusion is true. What is the general method for showing a syllogism invalid?

Show the answerHide the answer

The premises place both dogs and mammals inside the category of animals but say nothing about how dogs and mammals relate to each other. As the Euler circles on the right of Figure 11.1 show, the premises are equally consistent with an arrangement in which the dogs circle sits entirely outside the mammals circle, both inside animals. In that arrangement the conclusion fails, so it does not follow from the premises, even though in reality dogs are mammals and the conclusion is true. The general method is to find one arrangement consistent with all the premises in which the conclusion is false; you do not need to show the conclusion is actually false.

Question 4

For conditional syllogisms, which two forms are valid and which two are not? Give the invalid form that reasons “the street is wet, therefore it is raining”, and name it.

Show the answerHide the answer

Valid: modus ponens (if p then q; p; therefore q) and modus tollens (if p then q; not q; therefore not p). Invalid: affirming the consequent (if p then q; q; therefore p) and denying the antecedent (if p then q; not p; therefore not q). "If it rains the street gets wet; the street is wet; therefore it is raining" is affirming the consequent, invalid because the street could be wet for other reasons.

Question 5

Why do people fail the abstract Wason selection task but succeed with the drinking-age version? Give both explanations, and say what the contrast has in common with the analogical paradox of §10.7.

Show the answerHide the answer

In the abstract task (if vowel then even number, with cards E, K, 4 and 7), the correct answer is E and 7, since a vowel behind 7 would break the rule, the modus tollens move. Most people choose E and 4, and only about 4 per cent get it right. With the drinking-age rule (if drinking beer, over the legal age), about three-quarters correctly turn the beer card and the under-age card, although the logic is identical. One explanation is the permission schema: a lifetime of practice with rules of the form "to do X you must satisfy Y" guides the search. The other is an evolved cheater-detection ability tuned to spotting people who take a benefit without paying the cost. Like the analogical paradox, the contrast shows that abstract laboratory versions are hard while meaningful real-world versions of the same structure are easy: performance depends on the fit between the problem's presentation and the machinery brought to it.

Question 6

Distinguish the availability and representativeness heuristics, and give an example of base-rate neglect.

Show the answerHide the answer

The availability heuristic judges frequency or likelihood by how easily examples come to mind; the representativeness heuristic judges whether something belongs to a category by how much it resembles the category stereotype. Availability misleads when ease of recall reflects vividness or media coverage rather than frequency, as when snakebite seems deadlier than choking. Base-rate neglect: told that Steve is shy, tidy and detail-oriented, people judge him more likely to be a librarian than a farmer because he fits the librarian stereotype, ignoring the fact that farmers vastly outnumber librarians, so a random quiet man is still probably a farmer.

Question 7

Why can the conjunction fallacy never be a correct probability judgement? Explain in terms of what adding detail does to resemblance and to probability.

Show the answerHide the answer

The conjunction rule of probability states that the probability of two things being true together can never exceed the probability of either alone, because the conjunction is a subset of each component (feminist bank tellers are a subset of bank tellers). Adding detail, such as "and a feminist", can make a description resemble the person more, raising its representativeness, but every added condition can only keep the probability the same or reduce it. Judging the conjunction more probable follows resemblance instead of probability, and because of the subset relation it can never be correct.

Question 8

What does expected utility theory predict, and how do the Denes-Raj and Epstein jelly-bean results depart from it? Why does the fact that participants understood the probabilities make the result stronger evidence rather than weaker?

Show the answerHide the answer

Expected utility theory predicts that a rational chooser selects the option with the highest expected utility, here the small bowl, which gives a 10 per cent chance of winning against 7 per cent (or 9 per cent) in the large bowl. Many participants chose the large bowl with 7 red beans in 100, and a majority did when it held 9 in 100: the ratio bias, or denominator neglect. That they knew the probabilities were against them, and still reported feeling they had a better chance with more winning beans in view, makes the result stronger evidence. If they had misunderstood the arithmetic, the result would show only a calculation error. Because the arithmetic was understood and overridden, two processes must be competing for the decision, an intuitive one responding to the number of winners and a deliberate one that knows the odds, which supports the dual-system account.

Question 9

Explain the framing effect in terms of risk attitudes for gains versus losses, and give a real-world example of choice architecture exploiting status-quo bias.

Show the answerHide the answer

The same choice framed in terms of gains ("saves 200 people") makes people risk-averse, so they choose the sure option; framed in terms of losses ("400 people will die"), it makes them risk-seeking, so they choose the gamble to avoid a certain loss. This reversal reflects a value function that is concave for gains and convex for losses. Choice architecture exploiting status-quo bias: organ-donation rates are far higher in countries with an opt-out system, where you are a donor unless you decline, than in opt-in systems, because people tend to stick with the default; the same applies to automatic pension enrolment.

Question 10

Set out the homeostatic account of the difference between emotions and feelings, and explain how feelings could improve rather than degrade a decision.

Show the answerHide the answer

Every organism must keep its internal state within the narrow range that sustains life (homeostasis). On this account, emotions are the bodily responses that serve that regulation, such as changes in heart rate, gut and posture, while feelings are the conscious perception of how that regulation is going, a report on the state of the organism (Damasio, 2018). Because feelings track whether things are going well or badly for the body, they attach a value to experiences and anticipated outcomes, a rough tag of good-for-me or bad-for-me. That tag lets options be evaluated quickly without computing each one through, and lets the future consequences of actions weigh on present choices.

Question 11

Bechara and colleagues (1994) found their patients were guided by immediate prospects only. Explain why that impairs decision-making rather than making them more rational, and what it suggests about the relationship between emotion and reason.

Show the answerHide the answer

In the Iowa Gambling Task two decks give large immediate rewards but larger occasional penalties and lose money over time, while two give smaller rewards and come out ahead. Controls learned to favour the profitable decks; patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage did not, despite preserved intellect, and in their lives they repeatedly decided against their own interests. Being guided by immediate prospects only means ignoring future consequences, which is not rational: a rational chooser should weigh long-run outcomes. What the damage removed was the feeling-based value signal that makes the future weigh on the present. Emotion is therefore not the opposite of reason but part of what makes good decisions possible.

Question 12

Kermer and colleagues found the loss-gain asymmetry in forecasts but not in experience. Explain what that implies about loss aversion, and what mechanism the authors identified.

Show the answerHide the answer

Forecasters predicted that losing a gamble would hurt more than an equivalent win would please, even for a favourable gamble (50 per cent chance of winning $5 against losing $3), but those who actually lost were no less happy than those who won, and loss forecasters overestimated their unhappiness. Loss aversion is therefore at least partly an affective forecasting error: it operates on anticipated losses rather than on how losses are actually felt. The mechanism was that people failed to anticipate how readily they would rationalise a loss, for example reframing losing $3 of $5 as still having made $2; they overestimate how much they will dwell on a bad outcome and underestimate how quickly they will make peace with it.

Question 13

Describe Sanfey and colleagues' ultimatum-game findings. What does anterior-insula activity predict, and in what sense does rejecting an unfair offer stop looking irrational once repeated interactions are considered?

Show the answerHide the answer

In the ultimatum game a proposer splits a sum and the responder accepts, so both are paid, or rejects, so neither is paid. A purely payoff-maximising responder should accept any non-zero offer, but people routinely reject unfair offers. Sanfey and colleagues found unfair offers activated the anterior insula (associated with negative emotional states such as disgust), the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (cognitive control) and the anterior cingulate (conflict detection). The stronger the anterior-insula response to an unfair offer, the more likely the person was to reject it, while prefrontal activity favoured accepting the money. Rejection is irrational only if the immediate payoff is the whole story. In repeated interactions, a reputation for refusing to be exploited has value, because it deters future unfair offers, so the behaviour can be well adapted outside the one-shot laboratory game.

Question 14

State Gigerenzer's ecological-rationality argument. Why does the natural-frequencies result relocate part of the fault away from the reasoner?

Show the answerHide the answer

Gigerenzer argues that heuristics are not bugs but fast and frugal tools tuned to the environments in which they evolved and are used. Judged against abstract formal standards they look like failures, but judged by whether they produce good decisions quickly with limited information and time they often do very well, and simple rules can match or beat complex models on real problems. Many biases shrink or disappear when problems are posed in natural frequencies ("10 out of every 1,000 women") rather than probabilities ("0.01"), including base-rate neglect in medical diagnosis, even among practising doctors. If the error depends on the format of the question, part of the fault lies in the format rather than in the mind, which may be well suited to frequencies of observed events, the form in which information was naturally encountered.

Question 15

Using Table 11.1, name two effects from this chapter that are well replicated and two claims from the wider literature that are not. What distinguishes the two groups?

Show the answerHide the answer

Robust and well replicated: framing effects; base-rate neglect and the conjunction fallacy; belief bias in syllogistic reasoning; confirmation and myside bias; classic anchoring (any two). Not holding up or smaller than first claimed: ego depletion, which failed to replicate in a multi-laboratory pre-registered test (d = 0.04, 95% CI from −0.07 to 0.15); many social-priming effects, such as cleanliness cues altering moral judgement; and loss aversion, which is contested (any two of these). The robust effects tend to have a clear mechanism and a large, replicable behavioural signature. Those that shrank tend to be ones inferred from a small difference on a single measure.

Question 16

“People are fundamentally irrational.” Evaluate this claim using the material in §11.8, and say what a more defensible summary would be.

Show the answerHide the answer

The claim is too strong. The biases are real, and their direction is predictable, which is what makes them correctable, but many are smaller and more context-dependent than popular accounts suggest, and some (ego depletion, several social-priming effects) have not held up. Many errors shrink when information is presented in natural frequencies, so they partly reflect the format of the problem, and abstract tasks such as the Wason task are failed while meaningful versions with the same logic are solved. Heuristics produce good decisions quickly with limited information in real environments, and behaviour that looks irrational in a one-shot laboratory task, such as rejecting unfair offers, may be adaptive in the world. A more defensible summary is that human judgement is a system built to decide quickly and adequately with incomplete information, which mostly succeeds, and that rationality is a relation between a mind and its environment: good reasoning means recognising the specific situations where intuition reliably misleads and deliberately engaging slow thinking there.
