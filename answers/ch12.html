Drugs, Disorders and the Cognitive Brain

Week 12Chapter 12AnswersTutorial: TMS laboratory visit

Model answers to the 18 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 12. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbook

Question 1

A study finds that habitual coffee drinkers perform better after caffeine than after placebo. Why is that not yet evidence that caffeine enhances cognition, and what design would make it so?

Show the answerHide the answer

Habitual coffee drinkers tested after a night without caffeine are not at a neutral baseline; they are in mild withdrawal (tiredness, poorer concentration, headache). A dose of caffeine that removes the withdrawal will improve their performance relative to placebo even if it never lifts them above where they would be without the habit. This is withdrawal reversal. To show genuine enhancement you need a comparison with non-users or low users, or with habitual users who have not been deprived, tested on caffeine and placebo under double-blind conditions. Rogers and colleagues (2013) did this and found caffeine restored withdrawn consumers to normal, but did little for the alertness or performance of non-consumers beyond speeding motor responses: faster, but not smarter.

Question 2

Explain the difference between an agonist, an antagonist and a positive allosteric modulator, giving one drug example of each from this chapter.

Show the answerHide the answer

An agonist binds to a receptor and activates it, as nicotine does at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (opioids at opioid receptors, and psilocybin and LSD at 5-HT2A receptors, are further examples). An antagonist binds without activating, blocking the natural transmitter, as caffeine does at adenosine receptors (naloxone at opioid receptors is another). A positive allosteric modulator binds to a different site and amplifies the response to transmitter already present, as benzodiazepines do for GABA at GABA-A receptors.

Question 3

Why does regular caffeine use lead to withdrawal symptoms? Refer to what happens to adenosine receptors.

Show the answerHide the answer

Caffeine is an antagonist at adenosine receptors, blocking the sleepiness-promoting signal of adenosine. With regular exposure, cells compensate by expressing more adenosine receptors. When caffeine is withdrawn, as after a night without it, this enlarged receptor population is fully exposed to normal levels of adenosine, so the adenosine signal is stronger than usual, producing tiredness, poorer concentration and headache. The withdrawal symptoms are roughly the mirror image of the drug's effects.

Question 4

Using the inverted U, explain why a stimulant might help one student and impair another.

Show the answerHide the answer

Dopamine's effects on working memory and cognitive control follow an inverted U: performance is best at an intermediate level of dopamine signalling, and both too little and too much impair it. People differ in where they start. A student who starts below the optimum, for example one with low working-memory capacity, may be moved by a stimulant towards the peak and improve. A student who starts near the optimum may be pushed past it by the same dose, so their performance falls. Averaging across such people can make a drug look as if it does nothing.

Question 5

Bowman and colleagues (2023) found that “smart drugs” increased effort but reduced its quality. Explain how both can be true at once, and why the result matters for students.

Show the answerHide the answer

Effort and the quality of effort are different things. Bowman and colleagues found methylphenidate, dexamfetamine and modafinil all made participants spend longer and make more moves on knapsack problems, but the value of their solutions fell, apparently because their search became more random and less systematic. Stimulants change motivation (tasks feel more interesting and effort cheaper) more than capacity. Differences between participants also shrank and reversed, so people who did well on placebo tended to do worse on the drugs, consistent with the inverted U. For students this means the feeling of working harder on a non-prescribed stimulant is not evidence of working better, and those most tempted to take such drugs to do even better may have the most to lose.

Question 6

According to the alcohol myopia account, why might a drink make one person calmer and another more anxious?

Show the answerHide the answer

Alcohol myopia (Steele & Josephs, 1990) holds that alcohol narrows attention to the most salient cues in the immediate situation, while quieter, more abstract cues that take capacity to keep in mind drop out. Alcohol therefore has no fixed emotional effect: its effect depends on where attention is directed. A drinker left alone with a worry has nothing to distract them, so the worry dominates the narrowed window and they become more anxious. A drinker absorbed in a game or conversation has the worry pushed out of the window, so they feel calmer.

Question 7

Why can a person in an alcohol-related blackout hold a conversation they will never remember? Which stage of memory is affected?

Show the answerHide the answer

Alcohol primarily interferes with forming new long-term memories while leaving previously established memories and short-term maintenance intact (White, 2003). In a blackout the person can keep information active long enough to hold a conversation, find their way home or drive (which they should never do), but the information is never transferred into long-term storage, so it cannot later be retrieved. The stage affected is encoding (consolidation into long-term memory), not retrieval, linked to disrupted hippocampal activity, a pharmacological echo of anterograde amnesia.

Question 8

What would a confound have to look like to produce the Dunedin cannabis result?

Show the answerHide the answer

The confound would need to be present before cannabis use began, or at least not be a consequence of it. It would have to predict a decline in IQ relative to each person's own childhood score, not just a low IQ. It would have to grow stronger with more persistent use, to reproduce the dose-response relation. And it would have to survive adjustment for childhood socioeconomic status, low childhood self-control and family history of substance dependence. Candidates include early-emerging mental health problems, disrupted education or chronic poor sleep, if they both lead to persistent use and cause cognitive decline.

Question 9

Jackson and colleagues (2016) found no greater IQ decline in cannabis-using twins than in their non-using co-twins. What does this design control for that a birth-cohort study does not, and what can it not control for?

Show the answerHide the answer

Comparing a twin who used cannabis with a co-twin who did not controls for everything the twins share: their family environment (upbringing, socioeconomic status, schooling) and all or much of their genes. A birth-cohort study can adjust only for the factors it measured. The co-twin design cannot control for factors that differ between twins, such as different peer groups, mental health problems or experiences affecting only one twin, and its estimates become uncertain when few pairs differ sharply in use. In Jackson and colleagues' adolescent samples, use was mostly light and relatively short, so the design cannot rule out effects of the very heavy, prolonged use seen in the Dunedin long-term users.

Question 10

What does the REBUS model propose that psychedelics do, and how does it connect to the top-down and bottom-up processing discussed in Chapter 8?

Show the answerHide the answer

REBUS (relaxed beliefs under psychedelics; Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019) proposes that the brain constantly predicts its input, each prediction carrying a precision that determines how strongly it resists contrary evidence, and that psychedelics relax the precision of high-level prior beliefs. Bottom-up information then has more influence than usual. Perception becomes less constrained by expectation, which could explain vivid distortions, and deeply held beliefs about self and world become temporarily open to revision, the basis of the claimed therapeutic effect. This connects to the view in Chapter 8 that perception is a negotiation between top-down expectations and bottom-up sensory evidence: psychedelics shift the balance towards bottom-up evidence.

Question 11

Why is it so hard to run a properly blinded trial of a psychedelic drug, and why does it matter?

Show the answerHide the answer

Within an hour of taking psilocybin nearly everyone knows whether they have had it, and so usually do the therapists in the room, so the double blind breaks down. Once participants know their group, expectations can do much of the work: those who hoped for a breakthrough and received the drug may report improvement because they expect it, while those who realise they had placebo may be disappointed and report feeling worse. The comparison group is then not what it appears to be, so a difference between groups may reflect expectancy rather than the drug. This was among the concerns that led the US regulator to decline MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD.

Question 12

Lim and Dinges (2010) found that simple attention suffers more from sleep deprivation than reasoning does. Give one explanation of this counterintuitive result.

Show the answerHide the answer

Monotonous, simple tasks provide nothing to counteract mounting sleep pressure, so attention drifts and brief lapses, sometimes actual microsleeps, intrude. A complex, engaging reasoning task recruits effort and interest that can temporarily hold those lapses off. Simple sustained attention therefore suffers most, which matters because everyday tasks that depend on it, such as night driving on an empty road or monitoring a screen, are those where a single lapse can be catastrophic.

Question 13

What does general anaesthesia suggest about the neural basis of consciousness? Link your answer to §2.8.

Show the answerHide the answer

Anaesthetics act on varied molecular targets (propofol enhances GABA inhibition, ketamine blocks glutamate receptors) yet converge on unconsciousness, and unconsciousness does not require the cortex to fall silent: early sensory areas can still respond to stimulation. What appears to fail is integration, the communication between cortical areas and between cortex and thalamus that binds local processing into a unified experience. Ketamine also shows that responsiveness and experience can come apart. This fits §2.8, where awareness was said to depend on reciprocal, synchronised exchange across brain regions rather than on activity in any one area, as in recurrent-processing and global-workspace accounts.

Question 14

Compare the memory impairment typical of Alzheimer's disease with the cognitive impairment typical of Parkinson's disease.

Show the answerHide the answer

In Alzheimer's disease the earliest and most prominent deficit is in episodic memory, with rapid forgetting: learned information is lost abnormally quickly over a delay, and cues and recognition help little. This points to a failure of consolidation and storage, consistent with early hippocampal and medial temporal involvement; semantic, executive and visuospatial deficits follow as the disease spreads. In Parkinson's disease the core cognitive deficits are fronto-executive: cognitive flexibility, planning, working memory and learning from reinforcement, driven by disrupted dopamine signalling in striatal-prefrontal circuits. Memory problems there affect retrieval and strategic aspects more than storage, and a subgroup develops dementia.

Question 15

L-dopa improved task switching but impaired reversal learning in the same patients with Parkinson's disease (Cools et al., 2001). Use the inverted U and the uneven loss of dopamine to explain why.

Show the answerHide the answer

Dopamine loss in Parkinson's disease is uneven: it begins in the dorsal striatum and spares the ventral striatum until later. Under the inverted U, both too little and too much dopamine impair cognition. L-dopa restores dopamine in the depleted dorsal striatum, moving those circuits towards their optimum and improving task switching, which depends on them. The same dose overdoses the relatively intact ventral striatum, pushing it past its optimum and impairing probabilistic reversal learning, which depends on it. One medication therefore helps one function and harms another in the same person on the same day.

Question 16

Distinguish “wanting” from “liking”. How does the incentive-sensitisation theory explain why a person might crave a drug they no longer enjoy?

Show the answerHide the answer

Liking is the pleasurable impact of a reward; wanting is its motivational pull, the attraction of the reward and of the cues that predict it. Incentive-sensitisation theory (Robinson & Berridge, 1993) holds that dopamine supports wanting rather than liking. In some people repeated drug use makes the wanting system increasingly, perhaps permanently, hypersensitive to the drug and its associated cues, while the liking system is not sensitised. Wanting can therefore grow even as liking fades, so a person may crave a drug intensely while enjoying it less than ever, and cues such as places, people, equipment or times of day can trigger craving on their own.

Question 17

Why might a dose that a person has taken many times become dangerous in an unfamiliar setting? What does this suggest about the word “overdose”?

Show the answerHide the answer

Part of tolerance is learned through classical conditioning: cues that reliably precede a dose, such as a particular room or ritual, come to trigger a compensatory response that opposes the drug's effect before it arrives. In an unfamiliar setting those cues are absent, so the compensatory response does not occur and the body is unprepared for a dose it would usually tolerate, which can be fatal. Rats given a large heroin dose in a new environment were less likely to survive than those dosed in the usual one, and case reports describe the same in people. "Overdose" can therefore mislead: a dangerous dose is not simply a fixed amount but depends on the person's state and on the setting in which the drug is taken.

Question 18

Szigeti and colleagues (2021) had microdosers blind themselves. What problem does this design solve, and which problem from the blinding box in §12.6 might remain?

Show the answerHide the answer

It solves the problem of placebo control in a real-world practice that cannot easily be studied in the laboratory. Participants who were already microdosing packed their own doses and empty placebo capsules into identical opaque capsules and QR-coded envelopes, so they did not know which they were taking in a given week but the researchers could later decode it. This allowed a placebo-controlled comparison without supplying the drug, and showed that reported improvements in well-being and cognition occurred on placebo too. What might remain is the blinding problem of §12.6: if participants could detect when they had taken a real microdose from subtle effects, they could partly break the blind, and their expectations could then contribute to the reported effects.
