Chapter 8 answers

Visual Imagery

Model answers to the 28 self-check questions at the end of Chapter 8. Try each question yourself before you open its answer.

Questions in the textbookRead Chapter 8

Question 1

Distinguish visual from spatial imagery, and explain why a congenitally blind person is the clearest evidence that the two are separable.

Show the answerHide the answer

Visual imagery represents how something looks: its shape, colour and visual detail. Spatial imagery represents where things are and how they are arranged, their locations and relations, without necessarily any visual quality. A congenitally blind person has never had visual experience and so has no visual imagery, yet can have excellent spatial imagery, reasoning fluently about layouts and routes. One capacity is entirely absent while the other is intact, which shows they are separable. They also lean on partly different streams: the ventral "what" pathway for appearance and the dorsal "where" pathway for spatial relations.

Question 2

Why does the distinction between visual and spatial imagery complicate the claim that mental rotation shows images are “picture-like”?

Show the answerHide the answer

Mental rotation shows that transforming an image takes time in proportion to the angle, as if a real object were being rotated. But rotation is a transformation of spatial structure: it depends on the arrangement and orientation of parts, not on colour, texture or other visual detail. The result is therefore strictly evidence about spatial imagery, and could be produced by a spatial representation with no visual, picture-like quality at all. Milton and colleagues found that rotation and scanning tasks discriminate poorly between aphantasic and hyperphantasic people, which confirms that these tasks measure spatial imagery rather than visual experience.

Question 3

Explain Paivio's conceptual-peg account, and say why concrete nouns should benefit from it more than abstract ones.

Show the answerHide the answer

Paivio found that concrete nouns that readily evoke an image, such as magician, were remembered far better in paired-associate learning than abstract nouns such as discourse. On his conceptual-peg hypothesis, a concrete noun evokes an image that acts as a peg on which its associate can be hung. His later dual-coding theory proposed that a concrete word is stored twice, verbally and as an image, so retrieval has two routes instead of one. Concrete nouns benefit more because they readily evoke images, whereas abstract nouns have no obvious image, so they are stored mainly in the verbal code and lack the extra route.

Question 4

Shepard and Metzler found reaction time rose linearly with the angle of rotation. Explain why the depictive account predicts this, and why the linearity matters more than the slowing.

Show the answerHide the answer

On the depictive account, a mental image represents space with space, so rotating it means transforming it continuously through intermediate orientations, as with a real object. If this happens at a constant rate, the time required should rise linearly with the angle to be traversed, which is what Shepard and Metzler found. The linearity matters more than the slowing because a propositional system could be slower for larger angles for many reasons, for example because the problem is harder. What it does not obviously predict is a constant rate of transformation, which is the signature of an analogue process passing through intermediate states.

Question 5

Why does a propositional list of facts fail to predict the image-scanning result of Kosslyn, Ball and Reiser? Be precise about what a list lacks.

Show the answerHide the answer

Participants memorised a map of a fictional island, imagined it and mentally scanned from one landmark to another; scanning time rose in direct proportion to the real distance between landmarks. A propositional list such as "the hut is north of the well" encodes relations between landmarks but not metric distance, and a list is not traversed at a rate, so it contains nothing that would make one scan take longer than another in proportion to distance. A representation that preserves metric spatial structure, so that moving between distant points means crossing more intervening space, predicts exactly the observed timing.

Question 6

State Pylyshyn's tacit-knowledge critique and explain why it cannot be defeated by behavioural evidence alone. What kind of evidence does it require instead?

Show the answerHide the answer

Pylyshyn argued that imagery results need not reflect the format of the representation. Participants know how real objects behave and may unconsciously reproduce that knowledge, taking longer to rotate through larger angles or to scan longer distances because they know these take longer in reality, not because anything is being rotated or scanned. This cannot be defeated by behaviour alone because any timing pattern a depictive representation would produce is also one a well-informed participant could produce by simulating what they expect. It requires evidence that tacit knowledge could not fake, such as neural evidence: a retinotopic map recruited according to imagined size, a causal effect of disrupting visual cortex, or a reflex such as the pupillary light response to imagined brightness.

Question 7

Give one respect in which imagery resembles perception and one in which it clearly differs, and relate the difference to the Rider and the Elephant.

Show the answerHide the answer

Resemblance: imagery behaves like perception in fine detail. Details of an imagined object are easier to see when it is imagined large, just as with real viewing, and imagining a letter can prime its later recognition. Difference: perception is automatic and stable, while imagery is effortful and fragile, must be actively generated and maintained, and fades as soon as attention lapses; images are also harder to reinterpret than percepts. In the book's terms, perception is the Elephant's effortless delivery, supplied at no cost whether you attend or not, while sustained imagery is the Rider's laborious construction, which is why it is tiring in a way that looking is not.

Question 8

Explain how the TMS result of Kosslyn and colleagues (1999) does something the fMRI result of Le Bihan and colleagues (1993) cannot.

Show the answerHide the answer

Le Bihan and colleagues showed that primary visual cortex is active during both perception and imagery, but that is a correlation: it shows the region is engaged, not that imagery depends on it, and a participant simulating what they expect could also produce activity in visual areas. Kosslyn and colleagues applied disruptive TMS to early visual cortex and slowed both perception and imagery. Interfering with the region impaired imagery, so the region is doing part of the work: the contribution is causal. If imagery were purely propositional, disrupting visual cortex should not have slowed it.

Question 9

What does it mean to say early visual cortex is recruited retinotopically during imagery, and why is that pattern difficult for a propositional account to explain?

Show the answerHide the answer

Early visual areas are retinotopic: adjacent points in the visual field map to adjacent points on the cortex, with the centre of gaze at the occipital pole and the periphery further forward. Kosslyn and colleagues (1995) found that imagining a small object activated the posterior, foveal part of V1, while imagining a large object filling the mind's eye activated more anterior regions representing the periphery. So the cortical map is used in a size-appropriate way, as in perception. A propositional account struggles with this because there is no obvious reason why a list of descriptions should be laid out across a retinotopic map according to an imagined object's size.

Question 10

Patient M.G.S. could mentally approach a horse to within fifteen feet before surgery and only thirty-five feet afterwards. Explain why a smaller visual cortex produces a larger overflow distance rather than a smaller one.

Show the answerHide the answer

The mind's eye has a limited field, and an imagined object overflows it when it fills more than that field. Removing the right occipital lobe reduced the amount of visual cortex and so shrank M.G.S.'s imagined visual field. A smaller field is filled by a given object sooner, which happens at a greater imagined viewing distance. The distance at which the image overflows therefore became larger, from about fifteen to about thirty-five feet. The result ties the size of the mind's eye to the amount of visual cortex, in one patient measured twice.

Question 11

R.M. could draw an object in front of him but not from memory; C.K. could not recognise objects but could draw them from imagery. What does this double dissociation show, and what would a single case have failed to establish?

Show the answerHide the answer

R.M. had perception without imagery: he could draw an object placed in front of him but not from memory. C.K. had imagery without perception: with visual agnosia he could not recognise objects, even drawings he had made himself, yet could draw detailed objects from imagery. Each capacity is lost in one patient and spared in the other, so imagery and perception cannot depend on exactly the same mechanisms, though they share a good deal. A single case, such as R.M. alone, could be explained by imagery simply being the more demanding or fragile task, so that any damage to a shared system would impair it first. The reverse pattern in C.K. rules out that explanation.

Question 12

In Bisiach and Luzzatti's patients, the side of the square that was omitted changed with the imagined vantage point. Why does that show the knowledge was intact and the deficit was one of attention?

Show the answerHide the answer

Asked to imagine a familiar square from one end, the patients described only its right-hand side; imagining it from the opposite end, they described the side they had just omitted and omitted the side they had just described. Both halves of the square were reported at some point, so the knowledge of the whole square was plainly intact. What determined what was omitted was the left side relative to the imagined viewpoint, so the deficit was in attending to one side of a representation, not in the stored knowledge. The representation being neglected was an image.

Question 13

What is aphantasia, and why is it more informative for theory than a simple report of low imagery vividness would be?

Show the answerHide the answer

Aphantasia is the congenital absence of voluntary visual imagery (Zeman et al., 2015), reported by about four per cent of people: an aphantasic person can describe a banana's colour and shape perfectly well but reports seeing nothing in the mind's eye. It is more informative than a report of low vividness because it separates having visual information from having visual experience: the information is evidently present while the experience is absent. That makes it possible to ask what is missing, and to find, for example, that visual-area activity is largely preserved while connectivity is reduced. It also has an objective marker, the absence of the imagery pupillary light response, rather than resting on self-report alone.

Question 14

Milton and colleagues found that aphantasic, mid-range and hyperphantasic groups performed equally on standard laboratory memory tests but differed markedly on autobiographical memory and imagination. Explain what that combination rules out, and connect it to what §4.4 said about episodic memory and the self.

Show the answerHide the answer

Equal performance on standard laboratory recall and recognition tests rules out the idea that aphantasia is a general memory impairment. The graded differences (hyperphantasia above controls above aphantasia) on autobiographical memory and imagination, including imagining one's own future, show that what varies is the kind of remembering that requires projecting yourself into a constructed scene. Section 4.4 identified that autonoetic, self-locating recollection as the distinctive feature of episodic memory, and Chapter 4 suggested that remembering the past and imagining the future share machinery. The imagery literature arrives at the same conclusion: recollection and imagination share a system, and the sensory vividness of that system varies between people.

Question 15

Why is a three-group design comparing both extremes against a mid-range control stronger evidence than comparing aphantasic participants with everyone else?

Show the answerHide the answer

Comparing aphantasic participants with everyone else treats aphantasia as a deficit and shows only that one group differs from the rest, which could reflect something peculiar to that group. Comparing both extremes with a mid-range control tests the continuum itself: if hyperphantasic participants outperform controls, who outperform aphantasic participants, the relationship is graded and runs through the whole range of vividness. A graded, ordered pattern across three groups is much harder to explain by an incidental feature of one group, and it shows vividness is a spectrum rather than a dichotomy of normal versus impaired.

Question 16

Classic mental-rotation and scanning tasks discriminate poorly between aphantasic and hyperphantasic people. Why is that awkward for using those tasks as measures of visual imagery, and which distinction from §8.1 does it vindicate?

Show the answerHide the answer

If mental rotation and scanning measured visual imagery, people who report no visual experience at all should perform them very differently from people whose imagery is nearly perceptual. They perform much alike, so these tasks cannot be good measures of visual imagery experience. This vindicates the visual-versus-spatial distinction of §8.1: rotation and scanning draw on spatial imagery, which aphantasia can spare, rather than on visual imagery. It is why objective measures of visual imagery, such as the pupil response, were needed.

Question 17

Milton and colleagues measured connectivity at rest; Liu and colleagues measured it during an imagery task. Explain why agreement between two such different designs strengthens the conclusion more than a larger sample in either study alone would.

Show the answerHide the answer

The two studies differ in samples, scanners, field strength and, above all, condition: Milton and colleagues measured connectivity at rest with no task, finding stronger prefrontal-to-visual connectivity in hyperphantasia, while Liu and colleagues measured it during imagery, finding reduced connectivity between the Fusiform Imagery Node and frontoparietal areas in aphantasia. Each design has its own weaknesses and possible confounds. When designs with so little in common point to the same kind of frontal-to-visual coupling, it is very unlikely that a shared artefact explains both. A larger sample in one study would reduce random error but would not remove that study's systematic biases. Convergence matters especially because Liu and colleagues had only ten participants per group.

Question 18

In aphantasia, visual areas are active during imagery but connectivity between the Fusiform Imagery Node and frontoparietal networks is reduced. Why does this support the view that experience depends on integration rather than local activation? What does it imply about V1, and about the retinotopic findings in §8.4?

Show the answerHide the answer

If local activation of visual areas were sufficient for the experience, aphantasic people, whose visual-area activity during imagery is largely present, should have the experience. They do not, and what distinguishes them is reduced communication between the fusiform hub and frontoparietal networks. Experience therefore seems to depend on high-level visual cortex being integrated with frontoparietal networks, not on any one area being active: the parts are intact but the conversation between them is not (a functional disconnection). For V1, aphantasic participants show stronger deactivation of foveal V1, and patients with V1 lesions can retain vivid imagery, so V1 is not necessary for the experience. The retinotopic findings of §8.4 should therefore be read as showing that V1 contributes to imagery, especially vivid and detailed imagery, not that it generates the experience.

Question 19

How can the pupil be used as an objective index of imagery, what would you expect in an aphantasic participant, and why is a reflex better evidence here than a vividness rating?

Show the answerHide the answer

The pupil constricts reflexively to brightness, and Kay and colleagues found it also constricts more when people imagine a bright object than a dark one, so the difference between imagined-bright and imagined-dark trials indexes imagery. In an aphantasic participant you would expect no difference: the bright and dark imagery conditions should not separate, while the pupil still responds normally to real light and dilates with cognitive load. A reflex is better evidence than a vividness rating because the participant cannot produce it to order or suppress it to be helpful. It sidesteps the problem that vividness ratings are self-reports about something nobody else can check.

Question 20

In Kay and colleagues' task an eight-second black screen separated perception from imagery. What objection does that interval rule out, and why would the result be much weaker without it?

Show the answerHide the answer

Without the gap, the pupil might still be responding to the real stimulus when the imagery period began, and any brightness-dependent difference during imagery could be dismissed as a lingering after-image or a residual perceptual response. Eight seconds of black screen let any after-image fade and the pupil settle before imagery started. Without that interval, the critical result could have been explained without any imagery at all.

Question 21

The imagery pupil response is several times smaller than the perceptual one. Does that weaken the claim that imagery recruits perceptual machinery? Argue both ways.

Show the answerHide the answer

It weakens the claim if "recruits perceptual machinery" is taken to mean imagery is simply perception at reduced strength: during perception the bright and dark conditions separate by about three quarters of a millimetre, while during imagery they separate by about two tenths, so imagery does only a small fraction of what light does, consistent with other evidence that imagery and perception share some but not all machinery. It does not weaken it if the claim is that imagery engages the same pathway: a reflex that responds to imagined brightness at all, in a direction that matches perception and scales with trial-by-trial vividness, shows that the imagery signal reaches perceptual machinery. A smaller effect is what one would expect from an internally generated signal that is weaker than real input.

Question 22

The pupil response tracked trial-by-trial vividness ratings and an objective rivalry measure, but not questionnaire scores or average vividness ratings. What does that pattern suggest about the limits of questionnaire measures of inner experience?

Show the answerHide the answer

The pupil response agreed with measures taken trial by trial within a person (vividness ratings for individual images) and with an independent objective measure (binocular rivalry), but not with between-person self-report measures (questionnaire scores or average vividness across the experiment). This suggests questionnaires may not capture inner experience accurately, perhaps because people have no common scale for comparing their imagery with others', or because general self-descriptions differ from moment-to-moment reports. Studies that rest on people rating their own inner states should be treated with caution, and objective measures are needed.

Question 23

Aphantasic participants showed no imagery pupil response but normal perceptual pupil responses and normal load-related dilation. Explain why those two control findings are what turn an absence into evidence, and what could have been concluded without them.

Show the answerHide the answer

Without controls, an absent imagery pupil response could be explained trivially: aphantasic participants might have an impaired pupillary reflex, or they might not have been engaging with the task. Normal pupillary light responses to the real stimuli show that their pupils and reflex arc are intact. Normal dilation with greater cognitive load (four versus one triangle) shows they were engaging with the task. With those ruled out, the absence is specific to imagery, which turns a null result into positive evidence that a reflex that tracks imagined brightness in everyone else does not do so in aphantasia, the first physiological validation of the condition. Without the controls, nothing specific about imagery could have been concluded.

Question 24

Explain what motor imagery adds to the case that imagery reuses the machinery of perception and action. What does the relationship between imagined effort and corticospinal excitability show?

Show the answerHide the answer

Motor imagery, imagining a movement without making it, obeys the timing laws of real movement, so imagined and executed actions take similar durations, and it engages much of the same preparatory motor machinery. It extends the case beyond vision to action, suggesting that imagery generally is simulation using the systems built for perceiving and acting. TMS shows that motor imagery raises corticospinal excitability in primary motor cortex in proportion to imagined effort: imagining a forceful contraction drives the motor pathway more strongly than imagining a weak one (Helm et al., 2015). The imagined force is written into the excitability of the motor pathway, so imagining an action partly prepares it, which is why mental rehearsal is useful in sport and rehabilitation.

Question 25

Pearson and Kosslyn say the imagery debate should end. What do they mean, whose position is vindicated, and how does aphantasia fit their conclusion?

Show the answerHide the answer

They mean the question posed as a winner-take-all contest, pictures or propositions, is the wrong one. People use both formats and switch between them flexibly depending on the task. Both positions are partly vindicated: Kosslyn's, because depictive representations are real, functional and grounded in the visual system; and Pylyshyn's, because they are not the only currency of thought. The productive question is which format is recruited when, by whom and for what task. Aphantasia fits because an aphantasic person reasons about visual facts perfectly well by a non-depictive route. If depictive representation were the only way to hold visual knowledge, aphantasia would be a knowledge deficit; it is not.

Question 26

Explain why the method of loci works, using at least two ideas from this chapter and one from Chapter 5.

Show the answerHide the answer

The method of loci places vivid images of the items at successive locations along a familiar route and retrieves them by mentally walking the route. It forces elaborative, imageable encoding, giving each item a distinctive image (Paivio's imagery advantage). It recruits spatial as well as visual imagery, so the route provides an organised retrieval structure that preserves order. From Chapter 5: walking the route supplies a self-generated retrieval cue for each item, and self-generated cues are far more effective than cues supplied by others (Mäntylä); it also reflects organisation at encoding as an investment in retrieval, and encoding specificity, since the same route is reinstated at recall.

Question 27

Why must a pegword image be interactive rather than simply two images side by side?

Show the answerHide the answer

The peg works by providing a cue that retrieves the item. If the bun and the hat are imagined side by side, the two are stored as separate images with no link between them, so retrieving the peg (bun) does not bring back the item (hat). An interactive image, a bun wearing a hat, binds the two into a single integrated representation, so activating the peg retrieves the item. Integration, not mere co-occurrence, creates the association.

Question 28

The place-based technique studied by Reser and colleagues adds narrative to location. What does narrative contribute that place alone does not?

Show the answerHide the answer

Place supplies an organised set of locations, a spatial retrieval structure, but the items at successive locations are otherwise unconnected. Narrative adds causal and temporal structure: a story links each item to the next through events, so recalling one part of the sequence leads to the next through the story's logic. That is a second organising principle on top of place, giving memory more and more distinctive holds on the material. Reser and colleagues found the Aboriginal place-based narrative technique at least as effective as, and on some measures better than, the classical method of loci.
