Scenario 1
Deep Is Not Always Best: Levels of Processing Meets the Test
Why this matters for psychologists

Educational and clinical psychologists regularly advise people on how to learn: students preparing for exams, patients learning to manage a new condition, staff being trained in safety procedures. Levels of processing gives useful advice, but its limits matter just as much. Knowing that the best way to encode depends on how the information will be used later changes how training and revision should be designed.

Researchers replicated the design of Morris, Bransford and Franks (1977). Participants saw a series of words, each preceded by an orienting question. They were not told that their memory would be tested.

  • Semantic encoding: “Does the word fit this sentence? The ____ had a silver engine.” (TRAIN)
  • Rhyme encoding: “Does the word rhyme with RAIN?” (TRAIN)

Half the participants then took a standard recognition test (was this word presented?). The other half took a rhyme recognition test (did you see a word that rhymes with this one? for example, BRAIN). The graph shows corrected recognition (hits minus false alarms) for the items to which the answer at encoding was yes.

Easy
a) What does levels of processing theory predict for the standard recognition test? Why was it important that participants did not know their memory would be tested?

Craik and Lockhart proposed that memory is a by-product of how deeply a stimulus is analysed. Which question demands deeper analysis? Then ask what could go wrong if some participants tried harder to learn than others.

Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart, 1972) proposes that memory is a by-product of the depth at which an item is analysed. Judging whether a word fits a sentence engages its meaning (deep processing), while judging whether it rhymes engages only its sound (shallower processing). The theory predicts better recognition after semantic encoding, which the standard test shows (0.76 versus 0.52).

Learning was incidental: participants did not know a test was coming. This ensures that depth was driven entirely by the orienting question rather than by any intention to learn, so differences between conditions cannot be attributed to some participants trying harder or using their own memorisation strategies.

Easy
b) Describe the full pattern in the graph. Which result does levels of processing fail to predict?

Compare the two bars within each test. Is semantic encoding better on both?

On the standard test, semantic encoding beats rhyme encoding (0.76 versus 0.52), as levels of processing predicts. On the rhyme test, the pattern reverses: rhyme encoding beats semantic encoding (0.46 versus 0.29). The bars cross.

Levels of processing cannot predict the reversal. If deep encoding were simply better, semantic encoding should win on every test. The crossover shows that which encoding is best depends on what the test requires.

Medium
c) Levels of processing is often criticised as circular. Explain the criticism, and explain how transfer-appropriate processing avoids it.

How do we know a task produced deep processing? What would an independent measure of depth have to look like? Then ask whether transfer-appropriate processing labels whatever worked after the event, or predicts in advance which encoding will win on which test.

Circularity: we know a process was deep because it produced good memory, and we explain good memory by saying the process was deep. Without a measure of depth that is independent of the memory outcome, the theory risks explaining a result by redescribing it. Proposed independent measures, such as processing time or effort, do not track memory reliably.

Transfer-appropriate processing states that memory is best when the type of processing at encoding matches the type required at retrieval. It makes a directional prediction before the data are collected: semantic encoding should win on a meaning-based test, rhyme encoding on a sound-based test. That prediction could fail, so the theory is testable rather than circular. It also absorbs levels of processing as a special case: the “deep” advantage is what appears when the test demands meaning, which most standard tests do.

Medium
d) The graph shows only the items to which the answer at encoding was “yes”. Predict what the “no” items would show, and explain what this implies about how much weight the crossover can bear.

For a “no” item, the word does not fit the sentence or does not rhyme with the cue. Is the word integrated with its frame at encoding? Chapter 5 describes what Morris and colleagues found for these items.

For the “no” items, the textbook reports that semantic encoding won on both tests and there was no crossover. When a word does not fit the frame, it is judged against the sentence or the rhyme cue but not integrated with it, so the specific sound-based match that helps the rhyme test may not be formed in the same way.

The implication is that the crossover is real but conditional. It depends on the target having been integrated with its frame at encoding, and Morris and colleagues themselves noted that the role of congruency needed further clarification. Transfer-appropriate processing is not refuted, but its most cited demonstration holds under narrower conditions than the simple textbook version implies, so an argument resting on it should acknowledge the condition.

Hard
e) A third group used self-reference encoding (“Does this word describe you?”). Predict their performance on each test relative to the semantic group, and justify each prediction using both levels of processing and transfer-appropriate processing.

The self-reference effect is usually explained by the richness of the self-schema. Is self-reference a meaning-based or sound-based kind of processing? Which test asks for meaning, and which for sound?

Standard test: self-reference should produce recognition at least as good as, and probably better than, semantic encoding. Levels of processing treats self-reference as an even deeper level, because the self is the most richly elaborated structure we possess (Rogers et al., 1977), so relating a word to it creates many connections. Transfer-appropriate processing agrees, because self-reference is meaning-based and the standard test draws on memory for the words themselves, largely through their meaning.

Rhyme test: self-reference should do poorly, similar to or worse than the semantic group, and worse than rhyme encoding. Self-reference, like semantic encoding, focuses on meaning and says nothing about how the word sounds, so it does not prepare the participant to recognise a rhyme. Here the two theories diverge: pure levels of processing would predict the deepest encoding to be best on any test, whereas transfer-appropriate processing predicts that the deepest encoding will lose whenever the test demands a different kind of processing. The rhyme test result would therefore be a further test between the two accounts.

Applied
f) A student has an exam with two parts: multiple-choice questions on definitions and an essay asking them to apply theories to new scenarios. They plan to revise by highlighting the textbook and rereading it. Using this scenario and the textbook, advise them.

Transfer-appropriate processing says to practise the kind of processing the test will demand. What processing does each part of the exam require? Which strategies in §5.4 would match each?

Highlighting and rereading are shallow and passive. They produce a feeling of familiarity but do little to build the kind of memory either part of the exam requires. Transfer-appropriate processing says memory is best when encoding matches the demands of the test, so the student should revise in the format of each part.

For the multiple-choice definitions, they should practise retrieving and distinguishing terms: covering the definitions and generating them from memory, then testing themselves on similar terms that are easily confused. For the essay, they need to practise applying theories to new cases, not reciting them: writing short answers to practice scenarios, explaining how a theory predicts a result, and relating material to examples of their own (self-reference and elaboration).

Across both parts, they should test themselves rather than reread (the testing effect), space their sessions over days rather than cramming, and use their own notes and cues, since self-generated cues are far more effective than someone else’s. Practising in the format of the test matters more than practising hard.

Scenario 2
Testing as Learning: Retrieval Practice and the Illusion of Fluency
Why this matters for psychologists

The testing effect is one of the most practically valuable findings in cognitive psychology, and one of the least used. Educational psychologists, clinical educators and training designers use it to make learning stick, and understanding why learners prefer less effective strategies helps psychologists persuade people to change their habits.

Students learned a short science passage in four 5-minute periods, in one of three conditions:

  • SSSS: study, study, study, study (four rereadings).
  • SSST: study three times, then one free-recall test.
  • STTT: study once, then three free-recall tests.

No feedback was given on the tests. At the end of the learning phase, students rated how well they expected to remember the passage in a week (1 = not at all, 7 = very well). Mean ratings were 4.8 (SSSS), 4.2 (SSST) and 4.0 (STTT). Each student then took a final free-recall test, either 5 minutes or 1 week later.

Easy
a) Define the testing effect. Which group received the most retrieval practice, and which the most exposure to the passage?

Count the study periods and the test periods in each condition.

The testing effect (retrieval practice) is the finding that retrieving information strengthens memory more than an equivalent period of restudy. Testing is not just a measure of learning; it is one of the best ways of producing it.

STTT received the most retrieval practice (three tests) and the least exposure (one study period). SSSS received the most exposure (four readings) and no retrieval practice at all.

Easy
b) Describe the pattern of results across the two retention intervals.

Compare the order of the three bars at 5 minutes with their order at 1 week. Also compare how much each group lost.

At 5 minutes, repeated study was best: SSSS 83%, SSST 78%, STTT 71%. At 1 week, the order reversed: STTT 61%, SSST 56%, SSSS 40%.

The groups also differed in how much they forgot. SSSS lost 43 percentage points over the week, SSST 22 and STTT only 10. The more retrieval practice a group had, the less it forgot, so the advantage of testing appears at the delay that matters for real learning.

Medium
c) Students in the SSSS group expected to remember the most, but remembered the least a week later. Explain why rereading feels effective, and relate this to the spacing effect.

What does rereading something you have just read feel like? Chapter 5 describes fluency as a poor guide to durable learning. Spacing and testing both involve a certain kind of difficulty.

Rereading produces fluency: the passage becomes easier to process each time, and that ease feels like knowing. Students interpret fluency as evidence of learning, so the SSSS group gave the highest predictions (4.8). But fluency reflects how easily the text is recognised while it is in front of you, not how well it can be retrieved later without it. Their 5-minute advantage reflects this short-term familiarity, which fades quickly.

Retrieval practice feels harder and less successful, because each test reveals what cannot yet be recalled, so the STTT group predicted less. Yet that effortful retrieval is what strengthens memory. The spacing effect works the same way: distributing study forces partial forgetting and effortful re-retrieval between sessions, which strengthens the trace, while cramming feels efficient because it feels fluent. In both cases the strategy that feels best in the moment is a poor guide to what lasts.

Medium
d) Explain how the advantage of the STTT group at one week can be understood in terms of transfer-appropriate processing and encoding specificity.

What kind of processing does the final test demand? Which group practised that kind of processing during learning? What cues does free recall provide?

The final test is free recall: students must generate the passage’s ideas with no cues apart from the instruction to recall. Transfer-appropriate processing says memory is best when the processing at encoding matches the processing required at retrieval. The STTT group spent most of its learning time doing exactly what the final test demands, retrieving the passage from memory, whereas the SSSS group practised reading, which the final test never asks for.

From the angle of encoding specificity, each practice test lets students build and strengthen their own retrieval routes to the material: the cues and connections they will be relying on in the final test. Rereading builds familiarity with the text when the text is present, a cue that is absent at test. On both accounts, testing works because it rehearses the operation and the cues that the eventual test will require.

Hard
e) A sceptic argues that the testing groups did better at a week only because tests make students think harder, and that equally effortful restudy would do just as well. Explain how a design could address this, and why Karpicke and Roediger’s (2008) comparison of their Groups 2 and 3 already speaks against the sceptic.

A good design equates time and effort while varying only whether retrieval happens. In Karpicke and Roediger’s study, Group 2 stopped restudying items once they had been recalled, and Group 3 stopped testing items once they had been recalled. Which group lost out?

A design should hold total time constant and give the restudy group an equally demanding activity that does not involve retrieval, for example writing a summary with the passage in view, or answering questions with the text open, compared with answering the same questions from memory. If retrieval itself is the active ingredient, the closed-book group should still recall more a week later, despite equal time and comparable effort. Measuring effort (for example, self-reported effort or time on task) would let the researchers check that the groups were matched.

Karpicke and Roediger (2008) already provides strong evidence. Groups 2 and 3 both cut their workload. Group 2 dropped further study of pairs once those pairs had been recalled, and lost almost nothing a week later. Group 3 dropped further testing of recalled pairs, and recall fell by more than half. Both groups did less, yet only removing retrieval was costly. That is hard to explain as a general effect of effort or time, and it points to repeated retrieval as the ingredient doing the work, while repeated study of already-learned material added almost nothing.

Applied
f) A friend has three weeks before a final exam and plans to spend the last three days rereading their notes for ten hours a day. Using this scenario and §5.4 and §5.5, design a better plan and explain why it will feel worse but work better.

Use at least three findings: the testing effect, the spacing effect and self-generated cues. Then explain the gap between the SSSS group’s confidence and their recall.

A better plan spreads the work over all three weeks (the spacing effect), with short sessions on each topic revisited several times, and makes most sessions retrieval practice rather than rereading: closing the notes and writing down everything they can recall about a topic, answering practice questions, or explaining a concept aloud, and only then checking the notes to correct gaps. Items they get right should still be retrieved again in later sessions, since dropping repeated retrieval is what proved costly.

They should use their own cues: summaries and question prompts they write themselves, rather than a classmate’s notes, because self-generated cues match their own encoding (Mäntylä, 1986). And they should practise in the format of the exam, for example timed short answers if the exam requires them (transfer-appropriate processing).

The plan will feel worse: retrieval exposes what they cannot yet remember, and spaced sessions start with some forgetting, so it lacks the fluent, reassuring feel of rereading. The SSSS group in the experiment felt the most confident and remembered the least after a week. The sense of fluency produced by cramming is a poor guide to what will be remembered on the day of the exam.
